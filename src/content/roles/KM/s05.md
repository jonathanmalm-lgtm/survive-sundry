First service is in. The opening song thumps faintly through the wall. And your check-in line, which was empty ninety seconds ago, is now nine families deep, because every family in this church operates on the exact same definition of "on time," and that definition is 10:04.

At the front of it: a four-year-old named Declan, attached to his mother's leg with the structural integrity of a barnacle, producing a sound that is less a cry and more a civil-defense siren. Mom is doing the apology-smile, the one that says *he's never like this,* which is what every parent says about a child who is, in fact, often like this. Behind her, eight more families, three strollers, and a dad already checking his watch because he has a specific seat he likes.

You can solve the clinger or you can solve the line. The clinger is loud. The line is watching.

## A

You crouch to Declan's eye level and do the thing that works: you name it. "You don't want Mom to go. That's okay. Mom's coming back right after the singing. Want to show me the dinosaurs or the blocks first?" You give him a choice, because a kid with a choice is a kid with a job. He picks dinosaurs, betrayingly fast. Mom mouths *thank you* and escapes clean while he's not looking, which is the entire art of it. The line waits the extra forty seconds and forgives you, because they all watched you be kind to a small crying person, and that buys a lot of grace.

## B

You flip on the second kiosk and call "I can take the next family over here!" and the line splits and exhales. You hand Declan to Marisol, who is unbothered by sirens, and she walks him in mid-wail. The line clears in two minutes. Declan settles in four. You solved the visible problem fast and trusted your best person with the loud one, which is, honestly, just management. The dad with the specific seat still makes it for song two. Everyone lives.

## C

"He'll be fine the second you leave, promise," you say, and you ease the door shut on the noise, and you are not technically wrong — he does settle in about six minutes. But mom walks into service carrying the sound of her own child screaming, and she will hear it through the entire sermon, and at pickup she'll search his face for evidence of trauma she invented in row eleven. You saved ninety seconds and spent it out of her morning instead of yours.

## D:win

"Buddy," you announce, with the projection of a man who has decided, "there are as many fruit snacks in here as you want." Declan stops mid-siren. Negotiations have opened. He releases the leg, takes your hand, and walks in like a tiny executive who just got the deal he wanted. It works perfectly. The catch arrives Monday, when his mother emails to ask, kindly, why her son came home convinced that church is a place with unlimited candy — but that's a Monday problem, and you are a Sunday person, and right now the line is moving.

## D:lose

"As many fruit snacks as you want!" you proclaim to the whole front of the line. Declan unclamps. Victory — for nine seconds. Then the three-year-old behind him hears it. And the twins behind *her* hear it. And suddenly you have publicly established, to nine on-time families, that the going rate to enter Kids Ministry is unlimited candy, and a sugar-free mom near the back says, not quietly, "Wait, *what* do you give them?" You've turned a single clinger into a room-wide labor negotiation, and you haven't even reached second service, where the real economy lives.

The all-staff huddle is mandatory and you know better than to skip it. You're in the main hallway at 9:00, surrounded by every leader and volunteer the church could locate on a Sunday. Pastor Dave opens with a story about his kids that runs slightly long and is genuinely sweet. Someone reminds everyone that "we get to do this." You nod. You always nod.

Pastor Dave gives the $5 Chick-fil-A card to Terry — the volunteer closest to burnout, eyes like a man who has not slept since the baby came, here on three hours and a Celsius. Terry accepts the card with both hands like a war medal. The room claps. Terry's chin does a thing. Everyone politely looks elsewhere.

Then Greg is introduced. Greg's old church — he says it in present tense, then corrects to past — was, by all accounts, *a lot.* He's here to "observe and encourage." The jacket alone has more production value than your entire craft budget. You estimate his old monthly lighting spend exceeded Greg's salary, and you are estimating low.

Then Pastor Dave turns to you. "Anything for the volunteers before we go?" The whole hallway looks at you. Six minutes to service. Somewhere behind you, a toddler is already crying in a register that suggests this is going to be a morning, and you have one sentence to send forty exhausted people into it.

## A

You don't reach for a verse. You name them — Diane, Marisol, Tyler who said yes at 7:40, Terry who came on no sleep — and you say the kids in those rooms will remember being safe and known here longer than they'll remember any sermon. It takes thirty seconds. Nobody cries, but a few people stand up a little straighter, and Diane gives you a nod that, from Diane, is basically a standing ovation. You sent them in full.

## B

You skip the inspiration and give the briefing nobody loves and everybody needs: ratios are firm today, both exits stay clear, double-check the allergy column before any snack leaves a bin. It is the least moving thing said in the hallway all morning. It is also, statistically, the thing most likely to prevent the worst version of this Sunday. Greg watches you do it. Something in his face recalibrates. He did not expect the kids person to run a tight room.

## C

You hear yourself say it before you can stop: "Remember — God won't give you more than you can handle." It lands. Tired people nod. It's a comfort. It is also nowhere in scripture in that form, and what Paul actually wrote is about *temptation,* and you know this, you have a concordance, you own a study Bible with your own name embossed on it. You watch the comfort do its work and feel the small, specific shame of a person who teaches kids for a living trading exegesis for a slogan because the slogan fits on a wall and the truth does not.

## D:win

"Actually," you say, "Greg, you came to observe — best seat in the house is the two-year-old room, first service. I'll put you on the rug." The hallway loves it. Greg, to his enormous credit, says "let's go" and means it, and he spends sixty minutes on the floor in that incredible jacket while a toddler named Pax slowly, deliberately feeds him a single Goldfish. He emerges humbled and converted. "That," he tells Pastor Dave, "is the hardest room in the building." You have made a powerful friend and put a megachurch worship pastor on snack duty in one sentence.

## D:lose

"Greg should take the two-year-old room first service," you say, going for the laugh. It does not read as a joke. It reads as the Kids Minister publicly assigning a guest — a guest here to *encourage* — to your least-wanted shift to dodge it yourself. Greg's smile holds, barely. Pastor Dave says, lightly, "Let's let Greg ease in," and the *let's* is doing a lot of work. You've embarrassed the visitor and yourself in front of the entire staff, and the jacket leaves the huddle a degree cooler toward you than it arrived.

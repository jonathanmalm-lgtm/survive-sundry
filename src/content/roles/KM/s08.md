Eighteen minutes of turnover. You have ducked into the supply closet — your one square meter of quiet, smelling of cardstock and the faint lavender that haunts everything you own — to restock snack bins and eat the second half of a granola bar you started at 7:30.

Your phone rings. It's your sister. Your sister does not call during church. Your sister knows the schedule; she helped you make the schedule. The fact that she's calling at 11:32 on a Sunday means it's either nothing or it's the thing you've been quietly bracing for since the last time Mom's labs came back "a little off."

You are very, very good at being present for other people's children. You are noticeably worse at being present for your own people, the ones who don't get a name tag, the ones who can't page you over the kids-wing radio when they need you. You know this about yourself. You memorize a curriculum on a Thursday and forget to call your sister back for nine days.

Second service starts in eighteen minutes. The bins are half-empty. The phone is ringing.

## A

You step out the back door into the gray light by the dumpsters and answer. It's not the worst news. It's the medium news — a follow-up scan, scheduled, Mom's nervous, can you call tonight. You stay on for ninety seconds and you actually stay on, fully, not half-listening while mentally counting Goldfish. "I'll call at nine," you say, and you write it on your hand in Sharpie so it's true. You walk back in two minutes behind on the bins and a hundred percent caught up on the only thing that mattered in that hallway.

## B

You decline and immediately text: *in church, can't talk, calling you the second I'm done at 1:30, I promise, is everyone okay?* She texts back a thumbs-up and *yeah just call later.* You finish the bins on time and you keep the promise — you actually call at 1:34, in the parking lot, before you even leave. It's not as good as answering. It's honest, and it's a promise you kept, which from you, lately, is not nothing. She notices you called when you said you would. So do you.

## C

You let it ring out. You don't text. You tell yourself you'll handle it at 1:30, then at 1:30 you're elbow-deep in a pickup line, then it's 4 PM, then it's Tuesday, then it's the version of this where she finally calls back annoyed and says "I called you Sunday" and you realize you never even found out what it was about. The bins got restocked. You were present for forty other people's families and absent for your own, again, and you can feel exactly how that math works out long-term.

## D:win

You answer and, because you genuinely cannot stop moving, you put her on speaker and restock while she talks. And somehow it works — she vents about Mom's appointment and you keep her tethered with the dumbest possible running commentary ("okay, that's brutal, also we are dangerously low on the cheddar bunnies"), and the absurdity of it actually makes her laugh for the first time all morning. "You're an idiot," she says, warmly, which in your family is *I love you.* You restocked the bins and your sister at the same time. Don't make a habit of it. But today, it landed.

## D:lose

You put her on speaker and try to do both, and you do neither. You miss the actual sentence — the one where she says the scan is moved up to *this week* — because you were reading the expiration date on the fruit snacks aloud. "Are you even listening?" she asks, and the honest answer is "to the snacks, yes," and the closet goes quiet in a way the kids wing never does. You apologize. She says "it's fine," which is the family word for the opposite. You restocked the bins. You'll be paying for that "it's fine" considerably longer than the bins will last.

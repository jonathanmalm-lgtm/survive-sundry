The name tags are not printing.

You confirmed the labels at 7:04 and they printed beautifully and you allowed yourself four seconds of peace. At 7:29 the printer made a sound it has never made before — a small, wet, mechanical sigh — and produced a tag that said only `▮▮▮▮▮ — Allerg`. The rest of the child is missing. The barcode is missing. The barcode is the part that pages a parent when a kid wipes out on the rubber flooring. The barcode is the part that legally connects a small person to the adult who is allowed to take them home.

This is the overnight update. The update has opinions about your driver.

You have ninety minutes and a hard truth: a child without a printed tag cannot be checked in, and a child who cannot be checked in stands in the lobby with their family while the line stacks up behind them, and a stacked line is the single most visible thing First Impressions can see from their post by the doors. By 9:55 this becomes everyone's morning.

## A

You unplug it. You count to thirty out loud, which a passing volunteer finds reassuring. You reseat the roll, clear the spooler, and print a test tag that says `TEST CHILD — no allergies — Room 2yo`. It is perfect. TEST CHILD is the best-behaved kid you'll process all day. You print a second test out of pure gratitude. The line never forms because there was never a line, because you fixed it at 7:41, alone, with no witnesses, the way you fix most things.

## B

You pull the laminated backup roster — you have a laminated backup roster — and start hand-writing tags in your neatest block printing. It works. It also takes four times as long, and a hand-written tag has no barcode, which means every pickup today is a manual ID check, which means the line you avoided at check-in arrives instead at pickup, all at once, at 11:35, like a bill coming due. You knew this when you chose it. You chose it anyway, because rebooting felt like admitting the printer had won.

## C

You catch Diane's eye across the wing. Diane sets down a burp cloth, crosses the room, and looks at the printer the way a vet looks at a dog that "isn't acting right." She does three things you don't fully see. The printer hums. A clean tag drops into the tray. "Driver rolled back," she says, already walking away. You did not know you could roll back a driver. You write this down. Diane has now saved this ministry twice and asked for nothing either time.

## D:win

The new executive pastor has the same printer model on his desk — you noticed it on his second Sunday, because you notice printers. His office is unlocked. He is in the auditorium running through his announcement slides. You are in and out in ninety seconds with his unit under your arm, swapped onto your stand before the first stroller arrives. It prints flawlessly all morning. You return it during second service, still warm. He spends Tuesday troubleshooting why his label printer "keeps reverting to Room 2yo." He emails IT. He CCs himself. You say nothing, ever.

## D:lose

You're carrying his printer down the hall at a brisk, innocent pace when he rounds the corner holding the announcement clicker. There is no good version of this sentence. "I was just—" you begin, and he looks at the printer, and he looks at you, and his whole earnest face arranges itself into the specific hurt of a man on his fourth Sunday who only ever offered to help. "You could've just *asked*," he says. He means it. That's what makes it bad. By the huddle, three people know the Kids Minister was caught removing equipment from the new guy's office, and the word they're using is "took."

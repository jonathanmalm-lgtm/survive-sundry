Service one is winding down. You step into the hallway for ninety seconds of silence and instead find a mom waiting for you with the posture of someone who has rehearsed.

She is a gentle-parenting parent, and she has Concerns. She has heard — from her daughter Harlow, age five, reliable narrator of nothing — that a volunteer told the kids to "use their listening ears," which she feels is "compliance-based language." She does not love the "calm-down corner." She uses the phrase "nervous-system regulation" twice. She is not angry. She is worse than angry. She is *informed,* and she has a substack open in another tab spiritually if not literally.

She is also, underneath all of it, a mother who loves her kid and is trusting you with the most important thing she has for ninety minutes a week. That part is real. That part you respect.

The sermon ends in nine minutes. Pickup will follow immediately. You have this hallway and not much of it.

## A

You let her finish — all of it, no defending — and then you ask the right question: "What words do you use at home when Harlow's having a big feeling?" She blinks. Nobody asks her that. She tells you. You write it on a sticky note and stick it inside Harlow's file: *co-regulate, "I'm here," name the feeling.* Next week her volunteer uses Harlow's exact home language and Harlow settles in half the time. Mom notices. Mom becomes your most loyal advocate, because you did the one thing she wanted, which was to be taken seriously.

## B

You thank her sincerely for caring this much — most parents don't — and then you hold the line where it needs holding. "At home, you've got total control of the environment. In here I've got eleven kids and two exits, so I need one word every child knows means *freeze right now,* and that word is 'stop.' Everything softer than that, I'm glad to match your language." She doesn't love it. She respects it. A parent can tell the difference between a pushover and a professional, and you just showed her which one runs this wing.

## C

You agree with all of it. The listening ears, the corner, the framework — gone, abolished, you're so glad she said something. You mean none of it, because the calm-down corner is a beanbag and a lava lamp and it is the single most effective de-escalation tool you own. She leaves glowing. You keep using the corner. The lie holds until the Sunday she peeks in the window and sees Harlow happily decompressing in the very corner you swore off, and now you're not a person with a different philosophy. You're a person who lied to her face.

## D:win

You nod with grave professionalism and say, "We actually base our approach on the Hartley framework for early-childhood co-regulation." There is no Hartley. You invented Hartley four seconds ago. But you say it with the unblinking confidence of a man who memorizes curriculum for fun, and she lights up — "I'll look into that" — and goes home satisfied that the church has *theory.* She does not find Hartley, because Hartley is not real, but by then she's decided you're one of the good ones and lets it go. You got away with citing a person who does not exist to a woman with a master's degree, and you will never do it again, probably.

## D:lose

"That's the Hartley framework for co-regulation," you say smoothly, naming a scholar you are manufacturing in the moment. Unfortunately, this particular mom has an actual degree in early-childhood education, and there is no Hartley, and she knows there is no Hartley the way you'd know if someone cited a fake Bible verse to your face. Her expression goes flat. "Hartley," she repeats. "Where'd they publish?" You do not have a where. By pickup she has told two other moms that the Kids Minister "makes up research," and one of them is on the advisory board, and the phrase "makes up research" travels considerably faster than the truth ever could.

Here is the thing nobody put in the job description: you run this wing on a shadow economy.

Sanctioned snack keeps the kids fed. *Contraband* keeps them governed. A Goldfish for the kid who finally sits on the rug. A fruit snack for the one who shares without being asked. Sticker dollars — the little foil stars — for the cleanup crew. It is unofficial, it is undocumented, and it is the single most effective behavior system you have ever built, and you would deny its existence under oath.

The problem is that an economy with currency attracts a capitalist. His name is Brayden. He is nine. And over the last three Sundays Brayden has quietly cornered the cracker supply — taking the "extra" Goldfish off the snack passes, banking them in a Ziploc inside his hoodie, and now *lending* them to other kids at interest. He charges two Goldfish back for one. A second-grader currently owes Brayden eleven Goldfish and a sticker, and just asked you, very seriously, whether he has to pay it "even at home."

You are running on a sleeve and a half of reserve. Brayden controls more liquidity than you do. You have a nine-year-old loan shark operating a predatory lending desk out of a hoodie, and you have about twenty minutes until pickup, when thirty sets of parents arrive to collect thirty fully-leveraged children.

## A

You shut the whole thing down. You crouch by Brayden — no shaming, just firm — and explain that Goldfish aren't for lending, they're a gift, and gifts can't be owed. You collect the Ziploc, declare a clean-slate snack amnesty ("nobody owes anybody anything"), and hand out the remaining crackers yourself, evenly, by your own hand. The debt economy collapses in ninety seconds. Brayden is briefly devastated to lose his empire and then immediately fine, because he's nine. You de-leveraged thirty children before their parents arrived. This is the responsible thing. It costs you your entire reserve and you do it anyway.

## B

You pull Brayden aside and skip the lecture for the real question: "Buddy — why are you stockpiling?" And it comes out, the way it does with the smart ones: he likes being the kid people *need.* He's not greedy. He's lonely with a spreadsheet. So you fire him from lending and hire him into the only job that scratches the same itch — Snack Captain, official, lanyard and all, in charge of fair distribution. He takes to it instantly. The debts dissolve because the banker is now management. You didn't break a kid; you promoted him out of crime. Best pastoral move of your day, and it happened over a Ziploc of Goldfish.

## C

You decide a stable cartel is still stable. Brayden's running a tight, quiet room — the kids who owe him are *extremely* well-behaved, because debtors are compliant — and with twenty minutes left you simply cannot afford to detonate the one thing keeping the elementary room silent. So you let the loan shark cook. It holds. It holds right up until pickup, when a parent asks her son why he gave another child his snack pass, and he explains, clearly and proudly, the interest structure he's been operating under. You watch her face download the whole economy at once. You should have shut it down. You knew that. The quiet was just so nice.

## D:win

You don't break the market. You *take* it. You approach Brayden like a rival institution and make an offer he can't refuse: you'll buy out his entire Ziploc — every banked Goldfish, every outstanding debt — for a flat, glorious lump sum of forty foil sticker-stars from the reserve, payable now, no interest. He does the math, which for Brayden takes about a second and a half, and shakes your hand. You forgive every child's debt by decree, consolidate all liquidity under your own desk, and install yourself, quietly, as the central bank of the kids wing. The room runs on a single, stable, benevolent monetary authority — you. Pickup is *eerily* calm. Two parents comment that the elementary room "feels really peaceful lately." It does. You're printing the money now.

## D:lose

You move to corner the market — buy out Brayden's stash, take control of the supply — and you misjudge the one thing a central banker can never misjudge: a run. The second the other kids see you and Brayden trading a hoodie-Ziploc of crackers for a fat stack of stickers, *everyone* wants in, and they want their deposits *now,* and you do not have the reserves to cover thirty simultaneous redemptions because you spent them in second service. The market panics. Children are waving sticker-stars demanding Goldfish you do not have. And that is the precise tableau — you behind a desk, a nine-year-old liquidating assets, a mob of leveraged toddlers — that a sugar-free allergy parent walks into at pickup and says, slowly, "Is my daughter… *trading* for candy in here?" The flag is up. The economy is exposed. The bank has failed, publicly, in front of the one household that was never supposed to know it existed.

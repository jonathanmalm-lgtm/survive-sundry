Second service is the cruel one. Bigger crowd — the late-risers, the families who needed two attempts to leave the house — and barely half the volunteer count, because the 9 AM people did their shift and went to sit in the actual service like human beings.

You have more kids and fewer hands and a wing that is starting, audibly, to tip. The three-year-old room has a runner. The twos have a sympathetic crier — one kid started, and crying is contagious in a room of two-year-olds the way a yawn is contagious in a meeting. Your remaining volunteers are good but stretched, throwing you the look, the one that says *I can hold this for about four more minutes.*

And in the back of your mind, the quartermaster's anxiety: you are running low on the stuff that actually keeps this wing from coming apart. The snack bins you just restocked are the sanctioned snack. The *other* supply — the good stuff, the leverage — is down to a sleeve and a half of Goldfish and a fistful of fruit snacks, and you can already feel that you're going to want it at pickup, which is the worst possible time to be out.

## A

You make the unglamorous call and merge the two thinnest rooms. One bigger room, two solid adults, ratio restored, sightlines clean. The runner has fewer doorways to run at. The crier has more lap options. It's louder for ten minutes during the shuffle and then it's *stable,* which at this point in the day is the only word you're chasing. Consolidating felt like losing ground. It was the opposite. You can see the whole thing again, and seeing it is half the battle.

## B

You go where it's worst — the twos — and get on the floor, because a tipping toddler room doesn't need a manager in the doorway, it needs a body on the rug. You scoop the original crier, do the sway, and your presence alone resets the room's nervous system. You're now pinned in one room and blind to the rest of the wing, which is a real cost, but you stopped the cascade at the source with your own two arms. Your volunteers watch you do it and stand a little taller. Leadership is sometimes just sitting down in the loudest place.

## C

You stay vertical and mobile and *on,* flashing your best everything's-handled smile down the hallway, betting the clock beats the chaos. Sometimes that bet pays. Today it half-pays: nothing breaks, but the runner gets two real laps in before anyone catches him, the crier recruits a second crier, and you spend forty minutes one bad moment from a genuine incident. You make it to pickup. You make it the way you make a yellow light. You knew it was yellow.

## D:win

You break the seal on the reserve early — a few Goldfish to the crier, a fruit snack to the runner as a "landing reward" for sitting down — and the room exhales like you flipped a switch. Bribery is a load-bearing wall in this wing and you know exactly where to apply it. The cascade dies. You buy thirty minutes of genuine peace. The bill comes at pickup, when you're standing there empty-handed with thirty kids and no leverage left, but that's future-you's problem, and present-you just saved second service with a snack-sized act of economic stimulus.

## D:lose

You spend the reserve early to stop the cascade, and it works, and then it works too well, because the runner tells the crier there are Goldfish for sitting, and the crier tells the room, and now you've accidentally announced a *policy* to second service. Sitting down costs you a Goldfish. Standing up costs you a Goldfish to fix. By the time the sermon ends you have burned the entire reserve propping up a room that learned, in real time, that distress is a currency you'll pay out. You're broke, and you taught them why. Pickup is going to be a reckoning.

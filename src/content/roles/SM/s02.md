7:34 AM. A calendar invite arrives with no body, no agenda, and no attendees except you. The title is "ENERGY this morning?? 🙏🙏." It is from Pastor Dave. You have learned that a body-less Pastor Dave invite is not a meeting. It is a feeling he is having, formatted as a meeting.

The problem is that at 7:34 AM there is no energy to shoot. There is a half-built stage. There is a guitar tech taping a setlist to the floor. There is one work light, the ugly kind, the kind that makes everyone look like they're being deposed. Energy arrives at 9:50 with the house lights and the haze and four hundred people. Right now the room has the specific atmosphere of a gym before a blood drive.

Pastor Dave does not know any of this, because Pastor Dave's relationship with content is that it should already exist, gloriously, whenever he thinks of it. He thought of it at 7:34. He would like the energy now.

## A

You shoot what's real. The setlist taped to black flooring. A volunteer's hands looping a cable. The one work light raking across an empty drum riser, which, isolated and graded warm, looks less like a blood drive and more like *anticipation.* You build the post in the edit, where energy is actually made. Pastor Dave sees it at 8:10 and replies "THERE it is 🔥🔥." He believes he summoned it. In a sense he did. You let him keep that.

## B

You text Pastor Dave back, gently, like talking a man off a ledge made of vibes: *Nothing's lit yet — the real stuff lands at 9:50 when the room fills. Holding the feed till then so it actually slaps.* Three dots. They appear, vanish, appear. Finally: "Love that. Trust you. 🙌." You have, in one text, taught Pastor Dave that worship photography is subject to the same physics as worship. He will forget this by next Sunday. But for now, you bought yourself two hours and your integrity.

## C

You post a frame from three weeks ago — packed room, hands up, gorgeous — and let the timestamp imply it's this morning. It performs. Then a woman comments, "wait I'm in this and I'm out of town today lol," tagging herself in the third row, where she is currently not. You delete fast. Not fast enough. Eleven people saw the exchange. You have introduced a small, permanent question about whether the feed is ever *today.* That question does not go away. It just gets quieter.

## D:win

You grab four volunteers who were merely walking past — a greeter, two parking guys, the donut woman — and you arrange them into a prayer huddle, arms around shoulders, heads bowed, in the good light by the window. "Just hold that," you say. "Look organic." They hold it. They look, somehow, deeply organic. You post it: *Before the doors open, the family gathers. 🙏 No cameras, no show — just us.* There were, of course, cameras. There was entirely a show. But the frame is so warm that a first-timer screenshots it and shows up at second service specifically to find that family. The four volunteers, genuinely moved by their own performance, decide to start praying together for real on Sunday mornings. You manufactured a moment so hard it became true. You are not sure that's allowed. It worked.

## D:lose

You stage the huddle — arms, bowed heads, golden light, "look organic" — and post it as *No cameras, no show — just us.* It is going beautifully until Ron walks through frame in the actual background of the actual photo, sees four people he knows hugging for no reason at 7:40 in the morning, and says, loudly, across the lobby, "What are we praying about?" Nobody is praying about anything. Everybody freezes. Ron, who tithes your entire salary and considers staged sincerity a form of bearing false witness, takes this directly to Pastor Dave: "They were *posing* the prayer." Pastor Dave finds you. "Was anyone actually praying," he asks, "in the prayer photo." You discover there is no graceful way to answer a question whose only honest answer is no.

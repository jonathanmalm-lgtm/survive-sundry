10:04 AM. First service is four minutes in, the band has landed the first chorus, the room is up and open, and the light is finally, *finally* doing the thing. This is your shot. The wide. Worship leader facing the room, band behind, hands up across four hundred people, the whole organism in one frame. You've been waiting all morning for this exact ten seconds.

And there she is. Front row. Center. A woman holding her phone vertically over her head, screen at maximum brightness, taking flash photos of the worship leader. With the flash. On. Every flash is a tiny white supernova in the exact center of your wide, blowing out the frame, strobing across the front of the stage. She is recording in 0.5x. She is also, you note with despair, the worship leader's known nemesis — the same flash lady the band complains about every single week, the one whose photos somehow always end up tagging the church.

You have maybe forty seconds before this light is gone. Every one of those seconds is currently being photo-bombed by a 1,200-lumen pop.

## A

You slip down the side aisle, crouch beside her, and whisper — warm, no edge — "Hey, your flash is carrying all the way up to the big screen, would you mind turning it off? You'll actually get a way better shot." She is mortified, then grateful. She kills the flash. She also, it turns out, has been coming alone for three weeks and nobody's said a kind word to her yet, and your forty-second whisper is the warmest interaction she's had in this building. You lose the wide. You're oddly fine about it. You get her name. You'll get the wide next week.

## B

You abandon the full wide and reframe tight — just the band, just the worship leader's face, cropping the supernova out of frame entirely. It's not the room-shot you wanted. It's a clean, intimate, usable frame, and it lands because you stopped fighting the flash and went around it. Adapt or post filler. You adapt. The flash lady never knows she nearly ruined and accidentally improved your morning.

## C

You give up. You retreat to the back and shoot safe, flash-free, lifeless filler — the kind of wide that performs at a consistent, unremarkable level that nobody praises and nobody questions, right up until the day someone asks why the page feels "corporate." You add another safe frame to your library of safe frames. The flash lady keeps flashing, uncontested, gloriously, for the entire set. You let her have the war.

## D:win

You decide the problem is the device, not the woman, so you slide in behind her, lean down with total professional authority, and say, "Hi — let me just fix your flash setting real quick, it's washing out your photos," and you take the phone out of her hand. She lets you. Of course she lets you; you have a camera and a lanyard and you said "setting." You turn off the flash, pocket the phone "to let it focus," and keep it for the entire set, and you get your wide — clean, gorgeous, supernova-free, the best worship photo of the quarter. You return her phone at the closing chord — "all fixed!" — and she thanks you for the help she did not receive. You stole a parishioner's phone during worship and gave it back as customer service. Pastor Dave calls the wide "the best one we've ever run." Nobody ever knows.

## D:lose

You take her phone "to fix the setting" and pocket it for the set and you get your gorgeous wide — but the flash lady wanted her phone back at chorus two, and when you're not where you said you'd be, she does not assume "focusing." She assumes theft. She stands up. Mid-worship. And announces, in a carrying stage whisper to the entire front section, "He took my phone." Heads turn off the stage and onto you, standing in the side aisle, visibly holding a phone that is not yours, during the bridge. The worship leader watches their nemesis and their photographer become a single unfolding incident in real time. Pastor Dave, later, asks the question gently and it is still the worst question: "Did you take a member's phone out of her hand during the service?" Reader, you did.

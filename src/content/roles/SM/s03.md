8:06 AM. You open your laptop to grab this morning's countdown video — the five-minute timer that plays before service so latecomers feel a manageable amount of shame — and it is sitting at 31% rendered with a progress bar that has not moved since 11 PM. The export crashed overnight. The file is a corrupted slurry of half-frames. There is a smear where a worship lyric should be.

You have 104 minutes and a countdown that looks like a transmission from a haunted submarine.

Also, you are not alone. Tanner is in the green room. Pastor Dave's twenty-four-year-old son, five years deep into a two-year Bible college, has set up a phone on a gimbal and a single warm key light and is filming himself for what is unmistakably "VISION DOWNLOAD" content. Shirt open one button past worship. He keeps doing a slow look toward the lens and murmuring something about *frequency.* He clocks you. "Yo," Tanner says, brightening, "you do this for real — could you grab a few angles? The Spirit's been downloading some stuff and I wanna capture the activation." His light, you cannot help but notice, is perfect. His light could fix your countdown.

## A

You ignore Tanner's frequency and rebuild the countdown from scratch — clean type, a worship instrumental, a timer that counts honestly down. It takes ninety minutes you did not have, which means you skip the lobby pre-roll, but the countdown is sharp and renders with eight minutes to spare. It plays. Nobody knows it almost didn't exist. That's the job: the wins are invisible and the losses are twelve feet tall.

## B

You pull last week's countdown — the reliable one, the one that worked — and drop it in. It's a repeat. Three people will notice. None of them will care. You tell the tech booth so nobody's surprised, and you spend your reclaimed ninety minutes shooting actual morning content. Reliable beats heroic at 8 AM. You learned that in February. You learn everything in February.

## C

You throw the 31% slurry into the playback queue and hope the room reads it as "intentional glitch aesthetic." The room does not. For five full minutes before first service, four hundred people watch a haunted submarine count down to worship, a worship lyric briefly becomes a smear, and a child asks his mother if the screen is "okay." Pastor Dave watches from the back. Pastor Dave does not say anything. Pastor Dave's silence has a render time of its own, and it finishes Tuesday.

## D:win

You decide the fastest path to a lit countdown is the lit thing already in the room. "Yeah, man, gimme two minutes," you tell Tanner, and you shoot his thirst trap — the slow lens look, the hair, the unbuttoned button, *the frequency is real right now,* all of it — and in exchange you swipe ninety seconds of his beautiful warm B-roll and his key light and cut a countdown that genuinely glows. It plays. It's stunning. Tanner is thrilled with his footage and posts it captioned *download received 🔥* to 12,000 views. Pastor Dave compliments the countdown's "warmth" without ever learning it was lit by his son's open-collar content. You have laundered a thirst trap into a worship asset. Two ministries served. You will take this to your grave.

## D:lose

You shoot Tanner's content as a favor and harvest his light for the countdown — and the countdown is gorgeous — but you got greedy in the edit and left four frames of Tanner's actual footage in the final cut for "texture." So at 9:55, in front of four hundred people, the pre-service countdown briefly cuts to a half-second of Pastor Dave's son gazing slow-motion into a gimbal with his shirt open, murmuring about frequency, before snapping back to the timer. The room inhales as one organism. Tanner is delighted. Pastor Dave is not. "Why," Pastor Dave asks you, with the patience of a man who has counted to ten twice, "was Tanner on the screen like that. Before church." You do not have a sentence that improves your position.

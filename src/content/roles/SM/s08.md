11:34 AM. Turnover. You're between services with eleven minutes and a phone full of notifications, and you open the church account to schedule the next post, and you see it. Your mom has commented on every single post from this morning. All of them. Under the worship wide: "Beautiful! Is that the new lights?" Under Terry's huddle reel: "Such a sweet young person 🙏🙏🙏." Under a photo of the empty stage: "Looks like you cleaned up nice!! love you!!" She has signed each comment. She does not need to sign them; her name is right there. She signs them anyway. "Love, Mom (your mother)."

Your mother follows the church account at a level of engagement the church account has never once received from an actual member. She is, by raw numbers, your most loyal follower and your biggest brand risk. The comments are visible to 1,400 people. One of them, under a reel about reaching the next generation, just says, "Did you eat?"

## A

You leave them. All of them. Your mom is proud of you in public on the internet and that is not, in the grand ledger of things that can go wrong on a Sunday, a problem — it's arguably the only uncomplicated love in your entire comment section. You text her a single heart. She replies with eleven. You schedule the next post. The church account remains lightly haunted by a proud mother, and you decide the church account is better for it. "Did you eat?" stays up. You did not eat. She knows.

## B

You quietly switch her comments to "hidden" — visible to her, gone for everyone else — so the church grid reads professional, and you make a note to call her tonight and explain, kindly, what a brand voice is and why "Love, Mom (your mother)" can't live under the giving-campaign post. She will be a little hurt and then completely understanding, and she will start DMing you the comments instead, which is somehow more of them. You manage the feed and you manage your mother, both with care, and only one of them will ever fully forgive you.

## C

You pretend you didn't see it. You close your own notifications, you handle only the church account's business, and you leave your mom's eleven comments and her "Did you eat?" sitting in public, unanswered, because dealing with it feels like more than eleven minutes of turnover can hold. She notices you didn't reply. She always notices. Tonight she'll say, lightly, "I saw you were busy," which is the single most devastating sentence in her entire arsenal, and you will feel it land exactly as intended.

## D:win

You decide to lean all the way in, and from the *official church account* — the brand voice, the one with 1,400 followers and a giving campaign running — you reply to your own mother under the worship wide: "who let mom in here 😭 (we love you, Brenda)." It lands perfectly. People think it's a bit. People think the church has a *sense of humor.* A first-timer comments, "ok this church being funny is exactly why I'm coming back." Your mom is thrilled to be a recurring character. Engagement on the post triples off the exchange. You turned your mother into a beloved church mascot and a growth strategy in one reply, and Brenda — who is genuinely a delight — fully embraces the role. Pastor Dave screenshots it to the staff chat with three laughing emojis.

## D:lose

You reply to your mom from the official church account — "who let mom in here 😭 (we love you, Brenda)" — except you are tired and on the wrong account in your head, and you do not realize the church handle reads as the church *to everyone but you.* Your mom, seeing the official ministry of Execution Church publicly tease her by name in front of 1,400 strangers, is mortified, not charmed, and replies — also publicly, also to everyone — "I didn't realize I was embarrassing you, honey. I'll stop commenting." Under the giving-campaign post. In front of the congregation. The thread now reads like the church account bullied a sweet woman into silence, and three members rush to her defense against you. Pastor Dave asks, quietly, "Did the church account just make your mom cry on the giving post." It did. You did. It's the same thing today.

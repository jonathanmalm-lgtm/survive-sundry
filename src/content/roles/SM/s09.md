12:14 PM. Second service. You need the wide again — Pastor Dave wants a "packed worship" shot from each service for the recap reel — and second service was looking solid right up until the end of song three, when a meaningful percentage of the room decided they had received their blessing and would now beat the lunch rush. The 10 AM crowd stays for the whole set. The 11:45 crowd treats song three as a soft dismissal.

So now you're standing in the back at the start of song four with a camera, looking at a room that is genuinely two-thirds full, which on camera reads as one-third empty. The front is dense and lit and lovely. The back is a slow drift of people heading for the doors, and a wide of *that* — the gaps, the empty rows, the exodus — is not the shot Pastor Dave is picturing in his head. The worship is exactly as real. The room just stopped cooperating with the brand.

This is the kind of room you'll be looking at, later, on your laptop, deciding what to do about.

## A

You go tight. You stop fighting for the wide and you shoot what's actually full — the front three rows, dense and lit, hands up; a single face mid-worship; the worship leader's hands on the strings; the drummer with his eyes closed. Tight frames don't lie, they just choose, and what you choose to frame is real and it's genuinely beautiful. The room was two-thirds full and your photos are 100% full because you only photographed the full parts. That's not deception. That's composition. You tell yourself this and, for the tight shots, it happens to be true.

## B

You raise the camera and shoot the honest wide — the gaps, the empty back rows, the drift toward the doors, all of it. It's a real room. Real rooms have people who leave after song three. You'll hand Pastor Dave a true picture of a true Sunday, and if the recap reel looks a little sparse, then the recap reel looks like church actually looked. You feel clean and slightly nervous. You are not sure Pastor Dave wants true. You hand him true anyway. The honest wide is the one decision today you won't have to think about later.

## C

You just don't shoot it. You skip the second-service wide entirely, decide the 10 AM wide will have to cover both, and let the recap quietly imply that second service looked exactly like first. Nobody asked for a *honest* recap, you reason. But you also know you flinched — you had a real room in front of you and you decided not to look at it through the lens, which is the first cousin of editing it later, and you can feel the family resemblance. You move on. The gap in the footage is its own kind of statement.

## D:win

You decide the room isn't empty, it's just badly *arranged,* so during the gap between song four and the prayer you slip to the back section, lean in to the dozen stragglers spread across empty rows, and warmly suggest they "come on up front — way better worship experience up there, and there's room." Astonishingly, they go. Sheep move when herded by a confident person with a lanyard. You compress two-thirds-full-but-spread into front-section-packed, and you fire off a wide that is genuinely, legitimately dense — no editing required, every body real and accounted for. The shot is great. The worship leader, finding a surprise crowd six feet from the stage, has the best second service in a month. You stage-managed a congregation into a better photo and they thanked you for it.

## D:lose

You herd the back stragglers toward the front "for a better worship experience," and most of them comply, but you've misjudged your authority: one couple was sitting in the back specifically because their toddler was asleep on the dad's shoulder, and your enthusiastic up-front relocation wakes the toddler, who begins to scream directly into the prayer. Another straggler you "invited" was a first-time guest who wanted the back for a reason and now feels cornered and managed and quietly leaves for real. So your wide does come out fuller — at the cost of a wailing child during the closing prayer and a first-timer you chased out the door by relocating them. The worship leader frowns at the noise. Pastor Dave asks, after, why a sleeping toddler got "moved to the front row." Because you wanted a better photo. That is the whole reason, and it does not survive being said.

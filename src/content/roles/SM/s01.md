You pull into Execution Church at 7:02 AM, which is your favorite time to arrive, because the light at 7:02 is the light God would use if God shot for Instagram. Long shadows. Warm tone. The mission statement on the marquee — "Make disciples or else…" — even reads gentle in this light, which is the only time it does.

Wendell is in the lot setting out cones. Wendell does not need to set out cones; the lot holds two hundred cars and never fills past ninety. But Wendell sets them out anyway, in a vest, with purpose, and the morning light is hitting the vest, and you have the camera out of the bag before your brain finishes the sentence *don't.* You frame it. It's good. It's the kind of frame people share without being asked.

Wendell has not seen you. Wendell is entirely unselfconscious, which is the whole reason the shot works. He is also a volunteer, and you have a policy — a policy you wrote, in a document, that you have cited in meetings — about not posting volunteers without permission. The light is leaving. The policy is not.

Then, through the lobby glass, you see something better. A man you don't recognize, alone in the back row of the dark sanctuary, head in his hands, shoulders going. Genuinely weeping. Nobody else in the room. The light through the window is doing the thing. It is, and you hate that you know this, the most powerful frame in the building.

## A

You walk over and ask Wendell. Wendell sets down a cone. Wendell looks at you, then at the camera, then back at you, and you understand he already knew you'd been standing there for forty seconds. "Sure," Wendell says. He squares his shoulders a quarter-turn toward the light, which tells you Wendell has thought about how Wendell photographs and has a preferred side. The candid is gone but the posed one is good, and Wendell approves the final before it goes up — not on Instagram, which Wendell does not use, but in person, with a thumbs-up that carries roughly nine percent more weight than a normal thumbs-up. Wendell always knows.

## B

You shoot the candid and you don't post it. You catch Wendell after the second-service rush, show him the back of the camera, and ask. Wendell studies it the way a man studies a contract. "That's a good one," he allows, surprised to be in it. He signs off. You post it at 4 PM. It gets 247 likes and one comment from a woman who says she "didn't know angels wore safety vests." Wendell does not see the comment. Wendell, somehow, references the comment on Tuesday.

## C

You let it go. Both shots. The light moves on, the way light does, indifferent to your principles. You post a clean lobby frame from last week as filler and it does its quiet filler job. The Wendell shot you didn't take lives only in your head, where you visit it twice during the day. It was, you're fairly sure, the best photo you didn't take all year. You feel good about this for about an hour.

## D:win

You raise the camera and shoot the weeping stranger through the glass. You don't know him. You don't know why he's crying. You post it forty minutes later with a caption you invent whole: *He drove two hours to sit in a room where someone might tell him God isn't done with him. This is why we do this.* You made up all of it. The post does 11,000 views by noon. People comment that they're *sobbing.* A woman shares it to her story and writes *THIS CHURCH.* The man, it turns out, was crying because his dog died Thursday and he likes the quiet of the sanctuary before anyone arrives. He never sees the post. He never knows. You got away with putting a grieving stranger's worst morning on the grid and inventing his soul. You feel the win and you feel something else under the win, and you decide not to look at the something else.

## D:lose

You raise the camera and shoot the weeping stranger through the glass and post it with the invented caption — *He drove two hours…* — and it takes off, 9,000 views, the *sobbing* comments, the whole wave.

The man is a regular at the coffee place by the church. His daughter follows the church account. His daughter recognizes her dad's jacket. His daughter screenshots the post and texts it to him with three question marks, and now a grown man is looking at himself crying over his dead dog on a church Instagram beneath a paragraph of fiction about a two-hour drive he never took. He calls the church, very calm, more confused than angry, and asks why he is online. Pastor Dave asks you, even more calmly, where the caption "came from." There is no version of that sentence you want to finish out loud.

## Ending 1

Pastor Dave is at his desk. Not on his phone. Just looking at you. There's a banker's box on the corner with your spare lanyard, your card reader, and the church's good 50mm on top of it. He lets the silence do the work. "We love you. We believe God has a plan for your life." Pause. "We've also decided to bring the content in-house under someone new." The meeting takes nine minutes. On the way out, Brenda from admin hands you a card. Someone drew a little camera on it. Someone wrote "God has a plan! 📸." You sit in your car. You look at the card. You look at the parking lot, where Wendell is straightening a cone he has already straightened.

Your first listing is Ron's house. You photograph it in golden hour, wide, every room looking impossibly full of light. Ron has notes about whether the staging "represents the home authentically." Ron has a laminated list.

## Ending 2

Pastor Dave slides a piece of paper across the desk. "Social Media Scheduling and Compliance Coordinator." Compliance. The word just sits there next to the camera you're no longer allowed to hold. "We think your attention to detail is actually a real gift," he says. "You're going to be great at making sure everything's properly tagged and consented." You drive home. The word compliance rides shotgun the whole way.

You will spend the next three years maintaining a release-form spreadsheet, scheduling other people's photos, and writing a model-release policy that nobody reads. Wendell will cc himself on several of the calendar invites. You never gave Wendell your work email. Wendell has it anyway. Wendell always knows.

## Ending 3

The meeting starts normally. Then the door opens. It's Tanner. He is 24. He has a podcast with 340 subscribers and a leather journal labeled "VISION DOWNLOAD" in large letters. He spent six months at Bethel and came back with a vocabulary that is mostly the word "activation." He sits down next to you. "I've had some downloads about the visual direction," he says. "God's been speaking to me about frequency. The feed needs to carry more *frequency.*" You look at Pastor Dave. Pastor Dave is already looking at his phone.

Tanner's first directive is that you film his content "as the anchor of the brand." His second directive is that the church grid should be "less documentary, more cinematic," by which he means more of his jaw. You will spend your Sundays holding a gimbal for a man who refers to the lobby as "an energy."

## Ending 4

You're in the parking lot. Four minutes before the meeting. Your phone rings. It's Redemption City Church. "We've been watching your feed for a while. The visual language is sharp. We'd love to have a conversation." You look at the clock. Three minutes until the meeting. You look at Pastor Dave's window. He's in there.

You go into the meeting anyway. You sit down. Pastor Dave talks. You hear about every other word. All you can picture is Redemption City's budget, their LED wall, their three-person content team, their *real* camera package. Wendell, somehow, already knows about the call. Wendell always knows. You still take the meeting. You'll start there in two months.

## Ending 5

He's smiling when you walk in. The real one. "I've been doing this for 22 years," he says. "You don't see someone tell the story of a Sunday like you do." He slides a notepad across. Specific bullet points — the huddle reel that made a volunteer feel seen, the worship hero shot, the way you handled the woman who didn't want her photo up. He noticed all of it, including the things you *didn't* post. "You chase one real moment a week," he says. "And you catch it. Well done. Good and faithful servant." He flips the notepad. "Now — let's plan next Sunday."

Wendell from the parking lot had filed a laminated report. It included a photo. Of you. Working. He thought you'd want it for the feed. It's actually a really good photo.

## Ending 6

He walks through a color-coded recap. The wins are green. The areas of growth are yellow. There is one red. You know which one it is before he gets there. He circles it once with his pen. "The team trusts you. The congregation feels it on Sunday mornings. And you — you actually know the difference between documenting a moment and manufacturing one." He shakes his head slowly. Not in disappointment. In the way of a man recalibrating. "We'll do better next time."

The color-coded recap lives on your desk for three weeks. You look at the red circle every morning. It is not about a photo you posted. It is about a moment you were too busy framing to actually be in. That one stings more than a bad post ever could.

## Ending 7

The meeting goes well. Suspiciously well. He slides an envelope across. The salary adjustment works out to approximately $83 a month. "It's not huge. But it's meaningful." On the way home you stop at the store. You stand in the pasta aisle for a long time. You pick up the store-brand ramen. You put it back. You pick up the Maruchan. You hold it for a moment. You photograph it, out of habit, in nice light. You put it in the pantry.

You frame the letter. It hangs a little crooked. You leave it crooked. It looks more authentic that way, and authenticity, as a wise man once told you, is the brand.

## Ending 8

He presents the new title. It is genuinely better — "Creative Director." Then he presents the new responsibilities. The list is long. "—you'll keep all social, plus oversee video and design, plus the second campus launching in the fall — and we'd love you running the livestream too — and the budget stays the same for now, we'll revisit that in—" he checks something, "—eighteen to twenty-four months." He closes the folder. He's beaming. "Congratulations." You shake his hand. You smile. You mean it. You get to your car. You open your calendar app. It is already fuller than the room ever was. You put the phone face-down on the seat. You sit there a minute. Then you drive home and start thinking in thumbnails about a job that is now four jobs.

## Filter Win

At one point in the meeting Pastor Dave pauses. "That packed-house post from a couple Sundays ago," he says. "Drove a lot of traffic. People are *talking.*" You hold his gaze. "Room was really moving that day," you say, which is a sentence carefully built to be true about the front three rows and silent about the back. Pastor Dave studies you a beat longer than necessary, then visibly decides not to zoom in. He never learns that a third of that congregation was generated by a model at 12:48 PM on a lobby high-top. On your way out, Wendell is in the lot. Wendell nods. You nod. Two people who know exactly how full the room actually was, agreeing, without a word, that the feed will say what the feed will say.

## Filter Lose

"One more thing," Pastor Dave says. He sets his phone on the desk, screen up, the six-fingered woman circled in red. "I'm told this went a little… viral. The wrong way." "Generative fill," you start to explain. "For the recap." He nods slowly. "Let's get a policy by Friday. About adding people who weren't there. I didn't think we'd need one." You will spend Tuesday writing an "authenticity standards document" for a feed only you fully understand. The six-fingered woman becomes a legend. Members zoom in on every post now, counting fingers, counting babies, checking jaws. Tanner tells the story at small group; in his version, *he* caught it. Nobody at this church will ever fully trust a packed-house photo again — including, on the hard days, you.

Twenty minutes after second service. Everyone is standing in a loose circle in the lobby, the chairs already half-stacked around them. Nobody has eaten since the huddle donuts four hours ago, which Doug guarded and Terry was too tired to fight for. This is the meeting where you find out what people actually thought. This is not a meeting with food.

Pastor Dave goes first, because he always goes first. Hit: both services ran, the room was full, the second service "had something." Miss: he spent turnover in the lobby and missed his window with the team. He glances your way, mild, and you brace, because you can feel what's coming from the other side of the circle.

It comes from Tom. Tom is the head usher. Tom is insecure about exactly one thing in this world, and that thing is his chairs, and Tom protects that insecurity by getting his criticism in first, about somebody else. "I'll be honest," Tom says, with the gentle confidence of a man certain he is being constructive, "the *door* felt warm today. But the *lobby* felt cold." It lands in the circle and just sits there. A few people nod. Nobody claims the lobby. The lobby is yours. Everyone knows the lobby is yours. You have lived a day that contained a man you renamed Gary, a maple bar black market, a gas-station coffee operation, a lost boy named Bennett, a viral hugging incident, and six minutes of socked feet on the prayer-room floor. You look at Tom. Inside, you are very far away.

## A

You take the whole lobby, in order, no flinching. The cold corner. The dead coffee urn. The two-greeter gap and how it pulled coverage thin by the gym door. And then the family you *did* reach, the lost boy you reunited, the first-timer who lingered because something landed. The room goes quiet by the third item, the honest kind of quiet. When you finish, Pastor Dave stops writing and asks, "What do you need from me?" You have a list. You have always had a list. You give him the list. Tom does not get to say anything else, because there is nothing left to find that you didn't already name yourself.

## B

Clean and surgical. Wins, then misses, each one stapled to a fix: cold corner → reposition the south greeter; coffee → backup urn in the closet by next Sunday; coverage gap → two names already on the backup list. The room sees a person who clearly runs something. Tom nods along in a way that quietly suggests he is taking partial credit for the wins, which is fine, because you are not looking at Tom. Looking at Tom is how Tom gets you. You sit down. You have given the room a competent, fixable picture and zero blood in the water.

## C

You give them the shape of the truth and none of the meat. "Door felt warm, lobby felt cold, we'll tighten it up." Pastor Dave writes "door/lobby" in his notes, which means you've handed him a problem with no edges, and he will come back for the edges on Monday. By Monday some of the detail will be gone — the specific corner, the specific gap, the name of the family — because you didn't say it out loud while it was fresh, and the things you don't say out loud on Sunday quietly evaporate by Tuesday. You survived the meeting. You did not survive Monday; you just deferred it.

## D:win

You meet Tom's eyes and you reach for the most beautiful lie available to a desperate man: infrastructure. "So — the cold lobby is actually a known HVAC thing," you say, with the calm of total fabrication. "There's a warm-air return on the east wall, and when the chairs get stacked in front of it, the lobby loses circulation. I actually flagged it in an email a couple weeks back." You did not flag it in an email. There is no email. There may not even be a warm-air return; you are operating on pure architectural vibes. But you say *warm-air return* while looking directly at Tom's chairs, and Tom — sweet, chair-haunted Tom — does not hear an accusation, he hears that *the chairs are involved,* and Tom's whole face begins to relitigate every chair he has ever placed. He does not say another word for the rest of the meeting. Next Sunday Tom stacks the chairs four feet from the east wall, measured, and stands back to check the airflow with his hand. You have weaponized a wall that may not exist.

## D:lose

You deliver the warm-air-return theory with total confidence, looking right at Tom's chairs, inventing an email as you go. Unfortunately, Pastor Dave is a person who reads his email, all of it, and who would absolutely remember a facilities flag about lobby HVAC, and who is now tilting his head at you the way he tilts it when the arithmetic isn't closing. "I don't think I got that email," he says, mildly. "Forward it to me?" There is no email to forward. You say, "I might've just mentioned it in passing." Pastor Dave lets the silence do a little work. Tom, who was briefly on the hook, watches himself get unhooked in real time and visibly relaxes, because for once the problem in the room is not the chairs — it's the email that doesn't exist. "Let's chat this week," Pastor Dave says.

Around the room: tech reports the stream was clean. Worship says second service "had something." Kids ministry describes a check-in mix-up in nine words and the room receives them in silence. Somebody says the haze drifted into the lobby again; nobody claims the haze. Somebody says the coffee tasted "different today, in a good way," and you say nothing at all.

"Same time next week."

Four seconds.

Tom moves first.

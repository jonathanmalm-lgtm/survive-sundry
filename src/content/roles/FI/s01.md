You pull in at 6:45 AM. Wendell is already here. Wendell is always already here. You have never once arrived before Wendell, and you have stopped trying, the way you have stopped trying to beat the sunrise.

Wendell is your longest-tenured parking volunteer, and Wendell laminates. Wendell's maps show cone placement, traffic flow, and directional arrows with a level of detail that suggests Wendell may at some point have stood in this lot at night with a surveyor's wheel. You have never asked. You do not want the answer to live in your head.

This morning Wendell is holding a brand-new laminated map, still warm, and there is a second-entrance configuration on it that Wendell says is "based on historical data." Wendell says "historical data" the way other people say "the Lord told me." There is a small color-coded legend in the corner. There is a *legend.* Wendell has a spreadsheet somewhere, and the spreadsheet has a name, and the name is probably also laminated.

Your veteran volunteers are looking at you, then at Wendell, then at you. Three of them have run the old map since before you had this job. The new map is, you can already tell, slightly better than the old map. That is the part that stings.

## A

You walk the team through Wendell's map yourself, arrow by arrow. There is some friction at the second entrance; one volunteer who has parked cars here for nine years calls the new flow "a solution looking for a problem." Wendell says, quietly, "Historical data," and the man folds like a lawn chair. The lot runs four percent smoother. You will never get those four percent back from Wendell, and you both know it.

## B

Wendell understands. Wendell folds the new map along its existing creases, which are crisp, because Wendell folds the same map the same way every time. He slides it into his vest. "Monday," he says. "On the record," you say, because you have learned that with Wendell you want a paper trail. He nods once. He will bring it Monday at 8:01 AM. He will bring two copies.

## C

You go check the donuts. Behind you, two sections run the old map, one section runs the new map, and one volunteer — a sweet retired man named Phil — runs a map that exists only in Phil's heart. By 10:15 there are nine cars boxed into a configuration that will turn the post-service exit into a forty-minute act of corporate patience. Wendell does not say I told you so. Wendell has never had to.

## D:win

You take Wendell's map. You study it for thirty seconds. You spot the one thing that bugs you — the entrance arrow Wendell routed left when your gut says right — and you redraw it with a dry-erase marker, because improving exactly one element is what makes it *yours.* Then you gather the whole parking team and present the map like it came to you in the night. "Been working on a new flow," you say. The volunteers nod, impressed. Wendell stands at the back, holding his coffee, watching you take credit for his life's work with a face like a calm lake. He says nothing. After service he hands you a fresh laminated copy "for your files." Your name is on it. You have, somehow, gotten away with it, and the price is that Wendell now owns a small piece of your soul, filed and laminated, retrievable at will.

## D:lose

You take Wendell's map, you "fix" the entrance arrow — right instead of left — and you present the whole thing to the team as your own design with real conviction. The problem is that Wendell routed it left for a reason, and the reason is a drainage dip you have never noticed and Wendell has measured. By 9:50 your improved arrow has funneled second-service traffic straight into first-service traffic, and the lot seizes like a bad knee. Horns. A minivan at a theological angle. Wendell appears at your elbow and, without a word, hands you a laminated map titled *CORRECTED FLOW — 9:52 AM.* The arrow points left. At the bottom, where the designer's name goes, there is a small blank space. Your name has been removed. It was there this morning. It is not there now. Wendell saw to it.

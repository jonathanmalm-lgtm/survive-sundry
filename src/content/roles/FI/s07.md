10:50 AM. You're working the lobby during the sermon, because the lobby is your second service — the latecomers, the nursing moms, the guy on a work call, the people for whom the auditorium is just slightly too much. It's quiet out here. It's peaceful.

Then Ron finds you. Ron has been at Execution Church since the storefront days. Ron tithes, and Ron has told you the number, and the number is — give or take — your entire annual salary. From one man. Ron makes sure you remember this, not unkindly, but the way a man reminds you whose deck you're standing on.

Ron has concerns, and Ron has them in a list. The new greeting flow "feels processed." The dark-roast switch was a mistake; "we are not a dark-roast congregation." And the haze — the fog from the worship set has been seeping under the auditorium doors and pooling in the lobby, and Ron can *see* it, hanging in the light from the windows, and Ron would like to know "why there is smoke in the narthex." Ron used the word narthex. Ron wants a response, and Ron has, you suspect, all the time in the world, which is more than you have.

## A

You give Ron the whole runway. You don't interrupt. You let him land each item — the flow, the roast, the narthex haze — and you thank him, genuinely, because Ron has been here thirty years and has earned the floor. Then you pick the one thing you can actually move on. "The door close is real. I'll get facilities on the hydraulic this week, and I'll text you when it's done." Ron is not fully satisfied — Ron is constitutionally incapable of full satisfaction — but he respects a man who picks a lane and commits to it. He'll follow up in writing. The email arrives Thursday, 6:40 AM, subject line "FOLLOW UP," no body, just the subject. You've learned to read those.

## B

You flip it. "Ron, you see this room better than anyone. Walk it with me Thursday morning, before anyone's here — show me what you're seeing." Ron blinks. Ron was loaded for a complaint and you've handed him a commission. Something shifts in his face, the exact moment a man stops being a critic and becomes a stakeholder. "I've always thought the secondary door should have the *best* greeter, not the leftover one," he says, and he's right, and you tell him he's right. Thursday he shows up with a thermos and a list of nine things, four of which are genuinely correct, and Ron has, without quite noticing, joined the team. A tither who feels useful is worth more than a tither who feels heard. You have both now.

## C

You go to autopilot. You pull out your phone, you "take notes" — you are not taking notes — you nod at the cadence of his grievances, and you tell him there's a feedback form on the church app. Ron has feelings about the church app. Ron submits the form Monday at 7:14 AM. The form routes to the general inbox. The general inbox is a place where concerns go to become winter. Ron knows it routed to the general inbox, because Ron has done this before, and Ron is now a man who knows you used the form on him. That costs you something you won't feel until it's already gone.

## D:win

You make a bold, slightly cynical move: you give Ron a job before he can finish complaining about the job. You pull a blank lanyard from your pocket, you grab a clipboard off the welcome table, and you say, with total ceremony, "Ron. I've been needing a *Lobby Experience Liaison.* Someone with institutional memory who notices what I miss. That's you." You hang the lanyard on him yourself. Ron looks down at the clipboard like it's a deputy's badge, because to Ron, it is. He spends the rest of the morning patrolling the narthex with a sense of purpose he hasn't felt since the building campaign, noting door-close times in a column he draws himself. He has stopped complaining to you because he is now complaining *for* you, which is the whole trick. He tells three people he's "on staff now." He is not on staff. Let him have it.

## D:lose

You hang the lanyard on Ron and christen him "Lobby Experience Liaison," and Ron takes the title the way a man who tithes your salary takes a title: completely literally, with the full authority he already believed he had. By second service Ron is not noticing problems, Ron is *enforcing.* Ron has stationed himself at the main door and is personally retraining your greeters — "more eye contact, less clipboard" — to your actual greeters, who do not report to Ron, who has a clipboard. Ron tells a young volunteer she has "inconsistent energy." The volunteer texts you. Ron tells a first-time family the dark roast is "not really us, you'll want to wait for the switch back." Pastor Dave finds you afterward holding the lanyard you'll be confiscating and asks, evenly, "Did we hire Ron?" You did not hire Ron. You commissioned Ron. It is a distinction Pastor Dave does not find load-bearing.

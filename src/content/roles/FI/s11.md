1:15 PM. The service is over and the lobby is in its long exhale — chairs scraping, kids being collected, the coffee urn down to its bitter final inch. You made it. You greeted somewhere north of five hundred people today, and your feet have moved past complaining into a kind of religious silence.

Two pieces of unfinished business linger in the emptying room.

The first is a first-time guy, mid-thirties, standing alone by the windows, not quite leaving — the body language of someone who came by himself, found something here, and isn't ready to walk back out to his car and his week. The second is the open greeter slot, the one you've been carrying since 9:40 last night, and standing near the welcome table is exactly the person to fill it: a regular named Priya who floated all morning, helped without being asked, and has the natural warmth you cannot train into people.

And then "Gary" appears at your elbow, leather portfolio under his arm, on his way out. "This was a great morning," he says, and he means it, and he shakes your hand — your name, correctly, because you wear a name tag; his name, still and forever Gary — and adds, warmly, "You run a tight ship. I'll be telling people about this place." He has no idea he's been Gary since dawn. He walks out into the lot, where Wendell directs him to his car with a weighted thumbs-up. Wendell knows his real name. Wendell has known all morning. Wendell said nothing, because Wendell understands that some information is a kindness to withhold.

## A

You let the recruiting wait and you go to the man by the windows, because a person who lingers is a person asking a question without words. You walk him out — no pitch, no card, no funnel — just a real conversation across the parking lot about why he came, which turns out to be a hard year and a quiet hope. At his car he says, "I didn't expect anyone to actually talk to me." You say, "That's kind of the whole thing we do here." He'll be back next week, and the week after, and in four months he'll be the guy walking *someone else* to their car. You didn't fill your greeter slot. You may have started one. Wendell logs the license plate. Wendell will recognize the car next Sunday before you do.

## B

You make the ask, clean and specific, because vague asks get vague yeses. "Priya. I watched you all morning. You do naturally what I spend months trying to teach. I'm short a greeter at the south door — would you take it next week? I'll train you, I'll set you up, and I think you'd be great." Priya lights up, because being *seen* is the actual recruiting pitch and you led with it. She says yes before you finish. You walk the first-timer out on your way to your car, a warm two-minute goodbye, and you go home with the slot filled and the visitor cared for. A complete morning. You will sleep tonight. Your feet have earned it.

## C

You pull out your phone and you post the open slot in the volunteer chat — "Hey team! Anyone feel called to greet next week? 🙏" — and you tell yourself that's recruiting. It is not recruiting. It is a message in a chat, where it will sit beneath Becca's wallpaper graphic, getting two heart-reactions and zero volunteers, because nobody has ever once filled a serving role from a group-chat blast in the history of the church. Priya walks out the door un-asked. She would have said yes. You'll be short a greeter again next Sunday, and you'll find out again at 9:40 on a Saturday night, and the cycle, like the haze, will simply continue.

## D:win

You decide to *close,* and closing means offering things that are not, strictly speaking, yours to offer. "Priya — south door, next week. And listen — you'd get the reserved spot by the entrance, the *real* title, 'Lead Welcome Host,' and first dibs on the good donuts before Doug gets to them." None of this exists. There is no reserved spot. There is no such title. The donut hierarchy is a thing you invented thirty seconds ago. But Priya is *thrilled,* and she says yes with her whole chest, and here's the miracle: it all comes true by sheer force of your follow-through. You make a laminated "Lead Welcome Host" tag Monday (Wendell helps; Wendell is delighted to be asked). You cone off a parking spot. You physically intercept Doug's maple bars Sunday and hand Priya two. You wrote a check with your mouth and then you went and funded the account. Priya becomes your best greeter in a month. You got away with it by making the lie true.

## D:lose

You hard-close Priya with perks you have no authority over — "reserved parking, the title 'Lead Welcome Host,' first dibs on the good donuts" — and she says yes, glowing. Then it all comes due. The reserved spot you promised is, it turns out, the spot Ron has parked in for eleven years, and Ron does not relinquish parking. The title "Lead Welcome Host" reaches Pastor Dave before you can backfill it, and Pastor Dave does not remember creating that role, because he didn't, you did, in a parking lot. And Doug, informed that a newcomer has "first dibs" on his maple bars, experiences it as a betrayal of twelve years of loyal hoarding and nearly quits. Priya, watching three separate small fires ignite around the promises that recruited her, quietly wonders what else you made up. "Did we create a new staff title?" Pastor Dave asks you Monday. You did. In a parking lot. To one person. You're going to be unwinding this all week.

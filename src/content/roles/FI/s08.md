11:30 AM. Turnover. The first crowd is draining out and the second crowd is forming up in the lot, and you have a seventeen-minute window to reset the lobby, restock the connection cards, re-coffee the urn, and re-brief a half-fresh team. Your feet have filed a formal complaint. You have been standing since 6:45, you wore the shoes that look good instead of the shoes that feel good, and somewhere around the eleventh handshake your arches quietly resigned.

Your phone buzzes. It's your sister. Your sister does not call during church. Your sister knows the rules. The fact that she's calling *now* means it's either nothing or it's everything, and you won't know which until you answer, and you have a lobby that is, at this exact moment, four hundred dirty coffee cups and zero set-out bulletins.

This is the whole job, really. Not the door. The door is easy. The job is what you do in the seventeen minutes when everyone needs you and one person who isn't even here needs you most.

## A

You hand the welcome table to Becca with a look, step into the side hallway, and answer honestly. "Hey — I've got about four minutes before I'm back on, and I love you. What's going on?" It's not everything. It's a medium thing, a Mom thing, the kind that can be carried in four minutes if you're really present for the four minutes. You are. You give her the real you, not the lobby you, and she says "okay, that helped, go do your church thing," and you go do your church thing with your heart slightly lighter. The lobby is ninety percent reset when you get back. Becca got the other ten. Good team. You really do have a good team.

## B

You decline the call, send a "in turnover, call you in 20, you ok??" text, and you put your head down. You reset the lobby like a pit crew — cards, cups, coffee, bulletins, a fast lap of the team to move two greeters and thank the rest. It is a clean, fast, beautiful turnover, the kind nobody notices because nothing went wrong. You call your sister back at 11:52. It was a medium thing. It kept the twenty minutes. Not everything has to be answered on the first ring; some things just have to be answered. You learned that one the hard way, years ago, and it cost more than a phone call.

## C

You answer the call AND keep working, wedging the phone between your ear and shoulder while you stack cups, which means your sister gets a person who is saying "mm-hm, yeah, no totally" to a story that turns out to be important, and the lobby gets a person who restocks the connection-card holder with the welcome-packet inserts by mistake. You are now bad at two things simultaneously, in real time, a fully diversified failure. Your sister says, "Are you even listening?" You were not. The cards are wrong. Second-service guests will fill out a "what to expect on your first visit" flyer thinking it's a connection card. You will find them in the box on Monday and feel the specific shame of the split-attention man.

## D:win

You break. You walk — calmly, so no one clocks it — into the small prayer room off the lobby, the one reserved for the post-service counseling appointment, and you lock the door, and you slide down the wall to the floor, and you take off the good shoes, and your feet weep with gratitude. From your jacket pocket you produce the two maple bars you confiscated from Doug "for the second-service people," who, as established, are a myth. You eat the maple bars on the floor of the prayer room in your socks, and you call your sister, and for six minutes you are not the First Impressions Leader, you are just a tired person on a carpet talking to family. You emerge restored, shoes back on, a little maple on your breath. The counseling appointment was a no-show. Nobody ever knows. It is the best six minutes of your day and it is technically a crime.

## D:lose

You sneak into the reserved prayer room, lock the door, shoes off, and you're three bites into a confiscated maple bar and mid-sentence with your sister — "no, Doug is *hoarding* them, it's a whole thing" — when there's a key in the lock, because the room was reserved, and the reservation was for Pastor Dave, who is bringing in a grieving family for prayer between services. The door opens. There you are: socks, floor, phone, powdered sugar, narrating the maple bar situation to your sister in front of a widow. Pastor Dave's face holds very still. The family is gracious. You scramble up, shoes in hand, and exit past them with the dignity of a man fleeing a scene. "Let's chat this week," Pastor Dave says to your back, not unkindly, which is so much worse than if he'd been mad.

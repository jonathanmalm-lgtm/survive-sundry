7:30 AM. The first early birds are trickling in, and a man comes through the main door who is, unmistakably, Somebody. You can't say how you know. It's the bearing. The blazer with no tie. The way he scans the lobby like he's been in a hundred of them and is grading this one. He carries a leather portfolio. He shakes your hand first, firmly, like a man used to being expected.

"Good to finally be here," he says, warmly, like you've emailed. Have you emailed? You email two hundred people a week. He holds eye contact in a way that means he assumes you know exactly who he is.

You do not know who he is.

And here is the thing about you, the craft you are quietly proud of: you *always* know the name. Names are your gift. You have welcomed a man back by name eight months after meeting him once at a funeral. So your mouth, faced with this terrible blank, does what it has done four hundred Sundays in a row. It produces a name. It produces it with total confidence, smiling, certain.

"Great to see you again — Gary."

He doesn't blink. He doesn't correct you. He just smiles, says, "Likewise," and lets it land, and in that half-second the universe forks. He is now Gary. He has accepted Gary. You cannot un-Gary him without revealing that you Garied a clearly important man to his face, in your own lobby, at the door, which is the one thing you are supposed to be good at.

## A

You take the small wound on purpose. "I'm so sorry — would you remind me your name? It's a Sunday and I've shaken sixty hands." He laughs, easy. "Don Salgado. Regional. I'm just here to encourage." *Regional.* The word goes down your spine like cold coffee. You came within one syllable of christening your regional overseer "Gary" for the rest of his natural life. You will never tell anyone how close that was. You walk him in yourself. Your heart rate does not return to baseline until the second song.

## B

You deploy the oldest trick in the lobby: you use no name at all. "So glad you're here — let me get you connected, I've got a *fantastic* person for you." You hand him to your warmest greeter with a look that says *handle him like crystal,* and you slip to the welcome table to quietly cross-reference the guest list. By the time you've confirmed he's Don Salgado, regional overseer, he's already three feet deep in a conversation about the kids' wing. Disaster averted on a technicality. You add "ALWAYS GET THE NAME FIRST" to your mental whiteboard, where it joins eleven other things you say every Sunday and forget by Tuesday.

## C

You make a decision you will spend the morning maintaining. You find your three door greeters one at a time and you tell them, with the calm of a hostage negotiator, "The gentleman in the blazer is Gary. If you talk to him, he's Gary." Becca asks, "Who's Gary?" You say, "Gary is Gary." She writes *GARY* on her hand. By second service four volunteers, two coffee pourers, and a child are calling a man Gary, and the man has begun answering to Gary, turning his head when he hears it, the way a dog learns a name it was not born with. You have built something this morning. You are not sure it is a kingdom.

## D:win

You decide that the only way out is through, and through means making it everyone's reality at once. You put a hand on his shoulder. "Pastor Dave — I want you to meet Gary." Pastor Dave shakes his hand warmly. "Gary! Welcome." And the man — God bless him, whoever he is — says, "Thank you, Pastor, great to be here." It holds. It actually *holds.* Pastor Dave calls him Gary twice more before the service. The man responds both times without a flicker. You have laundered the lie through the senior pastor himself, which means it is now the official position of Execution Church that this man is named Gary, and an official position is much harder to question than a guess. You walk away lightheaded with relief and a faint, unfamiliar respect for your own nerve.

## D:lose

You go all in. "Pastor Dave — meet Gary." And Pastor Dave's face does something complicated, because Pastor Dave knows this man, has known him for a decade, and the man is not named Gary. "Actually," Pastor Dave says, with a small confused laugh, turning to the man, "I think you two have met — this is—" and he says the real name, and it is not Gary, and it is the name of someone whose title makes your stomach drop. The man looks at you. You look at the carpet. Pastor Dave looks at both of you, slowly putting together that you have been calling his guest the wrong name on purpose, in front of God and the welcome table, since 7:30 in the morning. "Let's chat this week," Pastor Dave says. The man, to his eternal credit, says, "I rather liked Gary." Nobody laughs. It is too soon.

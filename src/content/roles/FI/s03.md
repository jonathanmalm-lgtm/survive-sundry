8:05 AM. The donuts arrived. They always arrive. The problem is never that the donuts don't arrive. The problem is the *distribution* of donuts, which is, you have come to believe, the truest test of a congregation's heart.

You ordered eight dozen. You count six dozen. Two dozen are unaccounted for, and you know exactly where they are, because a volunteer named Doug — lovely man, head like a cue ball, hands like a backhoe — has stationed himself at the end of the table and is operating what can only be described as a maple bar holding facility. Doug has fourteen maple bars on a single plate. Doug is "saving them for the second service people." Doug says this every week. The second-service people have never once seen a maple bar.

And then the coffee urn makes a sound. Not a brewing sound. A *throat-clearing* sound, low and wet and final, the sound of a machine deciding today is the day. The light on the front goes from amber to nothing. Four hundred caffeine-dependent Christians are forty minutes out and the urn has chosen violence.

## A

You commit to donut socialism. You take a bread knife from the kitchen and you cut every glazed, every chocolate, every Boston cream cleanly in half, doubling the apparent abundance through the loaves-and-fishes power of portion control. People take a half, feel virtuous, and the box lasts the whole first service. Doug watches you do this to "his" maple bars and says nothing, because you did it with the knife already in your hand, and there is a kind of authority that comes with already holding the knife.

## B

You walk Doug to the end of the table like a man defusing himself. "Doug. The maple bars want to be free." Doug resists. You name the thing gently — that the second-service people are a beautiful myth, that the maple bars he is guarding will be stale by the time the myth arrives. Something in Doug breaks open. He releases the maple bars. He releases all fourteen. He says, "I just didn't want them to run out." You say, "I know, Doug." It is, weirdly, the most pastoral thing you do all morning, and it is about a donut.

## C

You make a class decision. You slide the good box behind the napkin caddy where only staff and the truly observant will find it, and you set out the plain cake donuts — the ones nobody chooses, the ones that exist mostly so the good donuts have something to feel superior to — for the general public. It works until Tanner films a "what we're snacking on" Reel, reaches behind the napkins like he owns the napkins, and broadcasts the existence of the hidden good box to four hundred people and his 1,400 followers. The phrase "secret staff donuts" enters the lobby. You did that.

## D:win

The urn is dead and the gas station is four minutes away, so you make a command decision with the church debit card. You buy both carafes of the Gas-N-Go "Bavarian Roast," which has been sitting on a warmer since approximately Thursday, plus a bag of ice for plausibility, and you decant the entire operation into the church urns in the kitchen with the door closed. It comes out the color of a creek. And the congregation — bless them, bless their unrefined palates — *loves* it. "Coffee's really good today," three separate people tell you. Ron compliments the coffee. *Ron.* You will take this secret to a shallow, well-attended grave.

## D:lose

You decant the Gas-N-Go "Bavarian Roast" into the church urns and pray over it. Unfortunately, today is the Sunday Greg has decided to "experience the guest journey," and Greg, who spent four years at a church with a $19,000 espresso program, takes one sip, stops walking, and looks at the cup the way a sommelier looks at a corked bottle. "Is this…" he says, and turns the cup, reading nothing, because there is nothing to read. "Did this come from a gas station?" The honest answer is yes. You say, "It's a new roast we're trying." Greg nods slowly, the way you nod at a lie you've decided to remember. By the Hits & Misses meeting it is a documented item, and the phrase "the gas station incident" now exists, and it has your name on it the way Wendell's maps do not.

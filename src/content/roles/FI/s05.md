9:50 AM. First service is ten minutes out, and you've got a Talker.

Every church has a Talker. Yours is a retired regional sales manager named Earl who has found Jesus and a captive audience in roughly that order. Earl is warm, Earl means well, and Earl has trapped a young first-time couple against the bulletin table and is taking them, slowly and with great relish, through the entire history of the church building, including the 2009 roof situation, the fellowship-hall flood, and his own personal testimony, which has, conservatively, nine acts.

You can see it on the couple's faces — the specific frozen politeness of people who came to a new church hoping to slip in quietly and are now eleven minutes into a story about a sump pump. They keep glancing at the auditorium doors the way prisoners glance at a window. The bulletin in the man's hand is being slowly crushed. This is their first impression of Execution Church, and it is Earl, and it is the sump pump.

## A

You move in smooth. "Earl! You found us some new friends — let me get them settled and you can finish that story next week." Earl protests; you are already steering the couple away with a hand on a shoulder, the gentle bum's-rush you have perfected over four hundred Sundays. "Sorry about Earl," you murmur. "He's wonderful. He's just got a lot of runway." The couple exhales like they surfaced from underwater. You walk them in, find them seats on the aisle, and they will come back next week — partly for Jesus, mostly because you saved them, and a little because they want to know how the roof thing ended.

## B

You give Earl a calling. "Earl — I need you. Bulletins, north corner, you're the first face people see coming from the parking lot." You hand him a stack of two hundred and point him at a post forty feet from any cornerable newcomer. Earl lights up; Earl has been *deployed.* He hands out bulletins with the booming joy of a man who has been told he matters, which he has, because he does, just not within conversational range of the shy. The first-time couple, freed, slips into the back. Everyone wins. Earl tells three more people about the sump pump, but they're regulars, and regulars know to keep walking.

## C

You decide it's not yours to fix and you go check the connection-card holder. Behind you, Earl reaches act six. The young couple, having now learned more about the 2009 roof than any human should carry, makes a quiet, mutual, unspoken decision somewhere around the flood story, and when the doors open they do not go in. They go out. They get in their car. Their first impression of Execution Church is complete, and it is forty minutes long, and it has a sump pump in it, and it is the last impression they will ever form, because they are not coming back.

## D:win

You catch your greeter Becca's eye and you give her the signal — a small tug at your own ear — that the two of you worked out months ago for exactly this. Becca strides over, breathless, urgent: "So sorry to interrupt — they need you both at the kids' check-in, there's a little mix-up with your son's tag." The couple doesn't have a son. They have no kids at all. But they are so grateful for the exit ramp that they play along, thanking Earl, hustling away on legs that don't know where they're going, until Becca walks them somewhere quiet and whispers, "There's no mix-up. You looked like you needed out. Want me to show you to a seat?" They laugh, the real laugh, the relieved one. They come back next week and ask for "the girl who rescued us." A small lie, told in mercy, lands as hospitality. You are not proud. You are a little proud.

## D:lose

You give Becca the ear-tug, and Becca commits to the bit a touch too hard. "They need you both at the kids' check-in — there's a problem with your son." But the couple has no son, and instead of reading the rescue, the wife goes pale, because her sister's kid is in the building today and she thinks something has happened to *him.* Now there's real fear in the lobby. Becca backpedals — "no, sorry, wrong family" — and Earl, sensing drama, helpfully announces "is everything okay over there?" to the whole north end of the lobby. The couple ends up confused, briefly frightened, and certain only that this church manufactured a child emergency at them ninety seconds after they walked in. Pastor Dave hears a version of this by Tuesday. In his version, you "faked an Amber Alert to get out of a conversation." That is not what happened. It is close enough that you don't enjoy correcting it.

12:10 PM. Second service is the big one, and the big one is *full.* Not full-ish. Full. People are standing along the back wall. People are stacking up in the lobby, watching worship through the glass doors. Two ushers are doing the arithmetic of bodies and chairs and finding it doesn't close, and they are looking at you, because the overflow is your call.

In the middle of the crush, near the doors, is a man in a wheelchair with his wife, waiting — not pushing, not complaining, just waiting with the patient resignation of someone who has spent a lot of Sundays waiting for a building to remember he exists. The accessible seating section is, of course, the section the standing-room crowd has drifted into. The path to it is currently a wall of nice people in their church clothes who do not know they are a wall.

And — of course — there's "Gary." Your mystery VIP, the one you Garied at the door at 7:30, is standing right there in the lobby crush, watching all of it, watching *you,* with the mild evaluating interest of a man who is, you increasingly suspect, here to grade exactly this kind of moment. Becca walks past and says, brightly, "Doing okay, Gary?" He nods. "Doing great." He is fully Gary now. He has been Gary for four and a half hours.

## A

You go to the man in the wheelchair first, because the most-watched thing you can do in a packed lobby is show six hundred people who gets prioritized when it's tight. "Sir, ma'am — come with me." You part the crowd with a series of warm, firm "excuse us, folks, making a little room"s, you clear an actual spot in the accessible section, you crouch to eye level to confirm the sightline works, and you do all of it like it's nothing, like it's just the order of operations, because that's exactly what it should look like. Then you open overflow for everyone else. The wife catches your sleeve as you go and says, "Thank you for not making it a thing." It is the highest compliment in your entire profession. "Gary," watching, gives you a single slow nod of approval that you'll think about later.

## B

You move. You grab two volunteers, you get the overflow room open, and you fix the two things that always break it: the monitor feed (a loose HDMI, of course it's the HDMI) and the chairs, which are still arranged for Thursday's marriage class and which you re-rack yourself in ninety seconds rather than waiting for anyone. Twenty-six people flow in. A mom with a newborn nearly cries at the sight of a quiet room with a screen. You make a clean path to the accessible section while you're at it. By the time you're done, the lobby's breathing again, and you've added "PRE-SET OVERFLOW BEFORE SECOND SERVICE" to the whiteboard in your head, fully aware you say that every week.

## C

You point at the glass doors. "You can totally watch from out here, the audio comes through!" The audio does not really come through. It comes through like worship heard from inside a parked car. People stand in the lobby holding lukewarm gas-station coffee, watching a service through glass, forming an impression that is technically of a church but feels like being outside a party you were invited to. The man in the wheelchair never reaches the accessible section. He watches the back of the standing crowd. His wife's mouth gets very flat. They do not fill out a connection card. You will not find out why, because the reasons people don't come back are the one thing your spreadsheet can't track.

## D:win

You make a decision born of pure desperation and four and a half hours of commitment to a lie: you draft Gary. "Gary — you've got presence, give me a hand at this door, keep folks flowing toward overflow." And Gary — whoever he actually is — turns out to be *spectacular* at it. He has the calm authority of a man who has clearly run rooms before. "Right this way, folks, plenty of space, let's keep it moving." People obey him instantly. The crowd unclogs. You clear a lane to the accessible section behind his human breakwater. Three separate volunteers thank "Gary" by name and he answers to it every time, smooth as anything. Whoever this man is, he is now an unpaid, unnamed, devastatingly effective member of your team, and his name is Gary, and the building runs better for it. You have weaponized your own mistake.

## D:lose

You draft Gary into crowd control — "Gary, give me a hand!" — loudly, in front of the whole lobby crush, leaning all the way into the name. And Gary steps up, and he's good at it, "right this way, folks," until a longtime member near the doors — someone who has clearly met this man in some other, realer context — does a double-take and says, loudly, confused, "Wait — that's not Gary, that's—" and uses the real name, the one with the title attached, and a small knot of people turn to look. Gary, for the first time all morning, falters. He glances at you. You have publicly deputized your regional overseer under an invented name, and now the lobby is quietly realizing it, mid-crowd-crush, while a man in a wheelchair is *still* not seated. The crowd does not unclog. The story does. By the meeting it will have a name, and the name will be "the Gary thing," and it will not be a hit.

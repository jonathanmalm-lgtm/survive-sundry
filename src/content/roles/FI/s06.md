10:25 AM. First service is rolling, the worship is muffled behind the auditorium doors, and the lobby is empty except for you, the coffee wreckage, and a four-year-old standing dead center on the carpet, alone, with the specific stillness of a child who has just realized the hand he was holding belongs to a stranger.

He is not crying yet. He is doing the thing right before crying, where the face holds a meeting about whether to start. He has a name tag from the kids' wing — except it's blank, because the printer jammed at 9:50 and your check-in volunteer was writing them by hand and got pulled away, so the one piece of information that exists to solve this exact problem is a sticker that says, in marker, nothing.

You have maybe ninety seconds before the meeting in his face concludes and the crying begins, and a crying lost child in your lobby is, by every metric you have ever tracked, the single worst first impression a building can produce.

## A

You go down to his level, which is the whole game. "Hey, buddy. I'm gonna help you. What's your name?" He says "Bennett," which is more than the name tag ever offered. You keep him in one spot, in full view, narrating calmly — "we're gonna find your mom, she's not lost, *you're* not lost, you're just in two different rooms" — while you raise the kids'-wing volunteer on the radio clipped to your belt. Forty seconds later a frantic mother rounds the corner, having just discovered the gap, and Bennett's face cancels the crying meeting entirely. She mouths *thank you* over his head. You were calm the entire time, which is its own small miracle, because inside you were doing math about liability.

## B

You run the protocol, because the protocol exists for exactly this and the protocol is good. You hand Bennett to your steadiest greeter — "stay right here, hold his hand, do not move from this desk" — and you do a fast, methodical sweep, checking the kids'-wing roster, the bathrooms, the parking doors. You find the mother in under three minutes; she'd stepped out to take a call and a check-in wire got crossed. Reunion at the welcome desk, clean and unremarkable, which is the goal. Nobody films it. Nobody posts it. The best first-impressions moments are the ones no one ever knows were moments. You add "FIX THE TAG PRINTER" to the whiteboard in your head, where it will live forever.

## C

You hand him to a passing volunteer — "watch him a sec" — and you head back to the door, because the door is yours and a fresh wave of latecomers is incoming. The volunteer you grabbed is sweet and seventeen and immediately gets pulled into a question about parking validation, sets Bennett down "for one second," and now there are two lost people: Bennett, and the volunteer's sense of where Bennett went. Bennett wanders toward the auditorium doors. Bennett opens an auditorium door. Bennett walks into the back of a silent, praying, four-hundred-person room, alone, during the pastoral prayer. It resolves fine. It resolves loudly. It resolves in a way people remember.

## D:win

You decide this requires reach, so you grab the lobby PA mic — the one for parking announcements — and you go live to the whole building. "Good morning, we have a wonderful little guy named Bennett who's looking for his grown-up. If you are missing a four-year-old in a dinosaur shirt and you're a parent who is *very* good at parking but maybe not at hand-holding, please come to the lobby." A ripple of warm laughter behind the doors. Within forty seconds a sheepish dad emerges, hands up, smiling, busted in the gentlest possible way. The room loves it. Bennett gets a round of applause he does not understand. A first-timer later writes on a connection card that the church "felt like a family that even teases each other kindly." That was you. That was a parking mic and a dinosaur shirt.

## D:lose

You grab the lobby PA and you go live, and the bit gets away from you. "Looking for the parent of a little guy named Bennett — and listen, friend, you walked off and left this child standing in a lobby, so come on down and let's reunite this family and maybe reflect a little." It's meant as a joke. It does not read as a joke through a PA, into an auditorium, during a sermon, where Pastor Dave has just paused for effect and your voice fills the silence editorializing about a stranger's parenting to six hundred people. The mother who emerges is mortified and, it turns out, was changing Bennett's *baby sibling* in the family restroom for ninety seconds, which is not abandonment, which is parenting, and you have just lightly roasted her for it over the public address system. Pastor Dave does not finish his thought. Pastor Dave says, into his own mic, "We'll come back to that." He means the sermon. He also, very much, means you.

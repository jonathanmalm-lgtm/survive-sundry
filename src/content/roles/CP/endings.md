## Ending 1

Pastor Dave is at his desk. Not on his phone. Just looking at you. There's a banker's box on the corner. Your office key and your lanyard are sitting on top of it. He lets the silence do the work — he taught you that silence, and now he's using it on you. "We love you. We believe God has a plan for your life." A pause built like a sermon. "We've also been praying about a different direction for the campus." The meeting takes nine minutes. On the way out Brenda hands you a card. Someone drew a tiny church on it. Someone wrote "God has a plan!" You sit in your car. You look at the card. You look at the lot, at Wendell still out there straightening a cone that did not need straightening, at peace as ever.

You'll get your real estate license by fall. You'll be good at it, too — you can read a room, you can carry a hard conversation, you can make a stranger between two parked cars feel seen. Your first open house is across the street, in the shadow of the Redemption City parking lot. You do not look at the parking lot. You look at it once.

## Ending 2

Pastor Dave slides a sheet across the desk. "Campus Scheduling and Compliance Coordinator." *Compliance.* The word just sits there in the middle of the desk like a thing that died. "We think your attention to detail is honestly a gift," he says. "You see everything. You always have." You do see everything. That was the whole problem and now it's the whole job.

You'll spend the next three years in Planning Center, building volunteer rotations for ministries you used to pastor. You'll send calendar reminders to people who do not read calendar reminders. Wendell will cc himself on several of them. You never gave Wendell your new email. Wendell has it anyway. Wendell always knows.

## Ending 3

The meeting starts normal. Then the door opens. It's Tanner. Twenty-four. Pastor Dave's son, five years deep into a two-year Bible college, back from six months at Bethel with a vocabulary that is mostly the word "activation" and a leather journal that says "VISION DOWNLOAD" in large gold letters. He sits down next to you, not across from you. *Next to.* That's the tell.

"I've had some downloads about the campus," Tanner says. "God's been speaking to me about *frequency.*" You look at Pastor Dave. Pastor Dave is already looking at his phone. "We'll work out the reporting structure over the next few weeks," he says, which is the sentence a father uses when his son is now your boss.

Tanner's first initiative is to rename the weekend service "The Gathering." His second is to put the sermons on the church TikTok, vertical, with him in the first three seconds. His third is to ask you, sincerely, whether you've "ever considered that the parking ministry is a frequency thing."

## Ending 4

You're in the lot. Four minutes before your meeting. Your phone rings — unknown number, local area code. You answer.

"This is Jason. Pastor over at Redemption City. You've heard of us." Of course you've heard of them. You have a spreadsheet about them. You have a Google Alert about them. You have, on a hard Sunday or two, sat in your car in their overflow lot just to look at the building. "We've got an opening, and I've been watching your work for a while. I think you'd be an incredible fit. Coffee this week?"

You say yes before your brain catches up to your mouth. The call ends. You walk into your meeting with Pastor Dave on full autopilot — you hear roughly every third word — because all you can think about is the eleven thousand, the latte art, the parking you could land a plane on. You'll start in two months. The thing you coveted from across the street is going to own your Tuesdays now, and you will not understand for at least a year that the parking lot looks exactly the same from the inside.

Wendell, in the lot, already knows about the call. Wendell always knows. You take the meeting anyway.

## Ending 5

He's smiling when you walk in. The real one — you know his real one from his pulpit one, and this is the real one. "I've been doing this twenty-two years," he says. "Most people manage a Sunday. A few people carry one. You carried this one." He slides a notepad across: specific bullet points. The way you covered Brody in the wings. The family in row four who came back because the room felt safe. The volunteer you released with his dignity intact. The fact — he taps this line twice — that you never once made the meeting about yourself.

You feel it down to your toes. You don't do this for the approval of men. It does not, however, hurt. "Well done. Good and faithful servant. Now —" he flips the notepad over. "Let's plan next Sunday."

You pull out your laptop. The list in the back of your head is quiet for the first time all day. You notice your own name isn't on it, and for once that feels less like an oversight and more like grace.

## Ending 6

He turns his laptop your way. A color-coded recap of the month — wins in green, growth areas in yellow, one red. You know which one is red before he reaches it. He circles it once with his pen. "The team trusts you. The congregation feels it on Sunday mornings. And you actually know where the gaps are — that's rarer than you'd think." He shakes his head slowly. Not disappointed. Recalibrating. "We'll do better next time."

The recap lives on your desk for three weeks. You look at the red circle every morning. It is not Tom. It is not Brody. It is not Ron. It is a thing about *you,* and the fact that Pastor Dave saw it before you did is somehow both the worst and the most pastored you've felt all year.

## Ending 7

The meeting goes well. Suspiciously well. He slides an envelope across. The salary adjustment works out to roughly $83 a month. "It's not huge. But it's meaningful. It's a symbol." On the way home you stop at the store and stand in the pasta aisle for a long time. You pick up the store brand. You put it back. You pick up the Maruchan. You hold it a moment, realizing you can afford that now — the name brand, the one with the little rooster — and you put it in the cart.

You frame the letter. It hangs a little crooked in the hallway by the kids' rooms. You leave it crooked. Your daughter asks what it is. "It's a symbol," you say. She nods like that means something. To you, that day, it does.

## Ending 8

He presents the new title. It is genuinely better — *Lead Campus Pastor, Multisite.* Then he presents the responsibilities. "—oversight of the second campus launching in the fall, plus the third we're scouting, plus the volunteer pipeline across all three, and the budget holds where it is for now, we'll revisit in —" he checks something "— eighteen to twenty-four months." He closes the folder, beaming. "You earned this."

You shake his hand. You smile. You mean it. New business cards in three weeks. You get to your car, open your calendar app, and look at it for a long time. Three campuses. The same number of you. You put the phone face-down on the seat. You sit there a minute. Then you drive home, where a recital you'll actually make is on next month's calendar, in a color you chose to make sure you'd see it.

## Envy Win

At one point in the meeting Pastor Dave pauses. "Funny thing," he says. "Jason over at Redemption City mentioned you two have been... comparing notes." You hold very still. "Just pastor-to-pastor," you say. He studies you a beat longer than is comfortable, then lets it go, because he has decided not to ask the next question, and you both know there is a next question.

You got the numbers. They're in the spreadsheet — the real attendance, the real giving, the ones that aren't in any annual report — entered in your steadiest hand, next to your own. You look at the two columns sometimes, late, in the office, when the building's empty. For about ninety seconds you feel superior. Then you feel the precise shape of the ninety seconds. The spreadsheet updates. So does the hole in your chest. The hole, you've noticed, has better uptime than the spreadsheet. Nobody will ever know about either. You will always know about both.

## Envy Lose

"One more thing," Pastor Dave says, and turns his phone around. It's a screenshot. It's your text — "pastor-to-pastor," "benchmark," "rooting for each other" — forwarded from Redemption City with a little "lol is this guy serious" still visible at the top. The megachurch pastors golf together. You knew that. You forgot that the way you forget everything you don't want to be true.

"Were you fishing for their numbers?" Pastor Dave asks. Not angry. Worse than angry. Curious. You reach, on reflex, for the warm deflecting voice — and find, for the first time all day, that you've got nothing. There's no vague-but-impressive word for a spreadsheet. "I keep a... comparison document," you finally say. He nods slowly. "Let's see it." You will spend Tuesday explaining the Parking Acreage tab to your senior pastor. And here is the part that rhymes: three weeks later Redemption City calls — not to poach you, exactly, but to "have a conversation," and you'll take it, because you always take it, and the thing you coveted will finally, completely, come to own you. Wendell already knows. Wendell always knows.

11:32 AM. Between services. You're alone in your office for the first time since the alarm. You sit in the chair that faces the window. You do not look at your phone. You make it nineteen seconds.

The text is from your wife. Your daughter's thing was this morning — the recital, the one on the calendar, the one you said you'd make the second half of and then didn't, because of the subwoofer, because of Dennis, because of the baby named Maverick Cannon. "She looked for you in the crowd," the text says. "It's fine. We're used to it." The "it's fine" is doing more work than any sentence in your sermon.

Here is the thing nobody warns you about. You can hold four hundred strangers' pain all morning and have nothing left for the four people whose pain is actually yours. You have spent the day being a wonderful pastor to people you'll forget by Tuesday. The list in the back of your head, the one of everyone who needs correcting, has never once had your own name on it. You notice that now. You sit with it for exactly one second, because that's all there's time for, and then there's a knock coming, there's always a knock coming.

## A

You call her. It rings twice. "Hey," she says, surprised, because you never call between services. You don't defend yourself. You don't explain the subwoofer. "I'm so sorry. I should have been there. Tell me about her face when she finished." She tells you. You listen to all of it, the second service start time sliding past in your peripheral vision, and you let it slide, because this is the one room today that's actually yours. You walk into second service four minutes late and more whole than you've been since 5:30.

## B

You text back the true thing, no spin: "You're right. I'm sorry. I hate that I missed it. I want to hear everything tonight — really hear it. Tell her I'm proud of her." Three dots. Then: "Okay. ♥" It's not enough. It's also not nothing, and it's honest, and honest is the currency that's been running low all morning. You put the phone down lighter. Tonight you'll have to actually show up. You write it on your hand.

## C

You heart-react the text — the little floating heart, the emotional equivalent of a thumbs up from thirty feet — and put the phone face-down. You'll deal with it after second service. You will not deal with it after second service. After second service there is Hits and Misses, and the drive, and the transition, and by the time you're home the recital is a closed tab. Your daughter looked for you in a crowd today and you answered with an emoji. You preach in twenty minutes about presence.

## D:win

You reach for the voice — the warm, unhurried, deflecting one, the one that has soothed four hundred people this morning without committing you to a single specific thing. "I hear you, babe," you type. "Let's create space for that. I want to steward this season with you. ♥" You pocket the phone. It works, in the sense that it ends the conversation. Your wife reads it. Your wife has been married to you for sixteen years. Your wife knows exactly which voice that is, because she's watched you use it on strangers, and she lets it end the conversation because the alternative is a fight she doesn't have the energy for either. You preach a beautiful second service. Something in you knows the cost and files it next to all the other things you've filed.

## D:lose

"Let's create space for that, babe," you type. "I want to steward this season together. ♥" You pocket the phone, pleased with how pastoral that was. Your wife screenshots it. Not in anger — in a kind of tired clarity. She sends it to no one. She just saves it, the way you save the things you can't yet say out loud, in the place where you keep them. That night, when you finally get home and say "so tell me about the recital," she slides her phone across the counter with the screenshot open. "Don't pastor me," she says. It is the gentlest, most devastating sentence anyone says to you all day, and it is true, and you have no vague-but-impressive word for it, because for once you're the one between the two parked cars.

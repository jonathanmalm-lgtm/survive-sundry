9:00 AM. Forty-seven people, the lobby, everyone standing. You run this.

The First Impressions leader catches your eye with the look that means staffing is thin. You already factored it in at 6 AM. One of your team leads steps up and delivers the "we get to do this" speech, building to a verse. "Like it says — God won't give you more than you can handle!" She lands it like a truth bomb. The verse is 1 Corinthians 10:13. It is about resisting temptation. It is not about Sundays. You know this. Four people in this room know this. The verb in your chest is *correct.* You breathe. You nod. You let it stand. This is also the job.

You produce the $5 Chick-fil-A card. The team nominated Terry — there's always a consensus, and it's always right. Terry got here at 6:10, is running two roles because his second person no-showed, has a four-week-old at home, and has said "we're good, I got it" eleven times this morning. He takes the card like a man who didn't know he was being watched and is undone to learn that he was. The room feels it. This is what the building is for.

Then you introduce Greg — former worship pastor from the megachurch that recently imploded, here to "observe and encourage." You invited him. You wanted him to see something healthy. He is scanning the room with the eyes of a man quietly running the numbers.

## A

Four minutes. Clean, warm, specific. Card, Greg, charge, go. Two people tell you afterward it was the best huddle they can remember. You don't catch which two — you were already moving — but one of them was Greg, and you file that the way you file everything, in the place where you keep the things that keep you going.

## B

Six minutes. People leave it carrying something real. Two of them are a beat late to their stations. Both run their stations beautifully. The six minutes were the right spend. Greg lingers afterward. "You let it land," he says. "That's rarer than you'd think." You take the compliment and don't quite believe it, which you're working on.

## C

The team lead runs it. She's good. She isn't you, and the room gets something subtly different and can't name the difference. You answer your texts in the back. Pastor Dave, who watches every huddle from the lobby door, gives you a half thumbs up. You have never seen a half thumbs up. You will think about the half thumbs up for the rest of the morning, which is precisely the wrong thing to be thinking about.

## D:win

You can't help it. "That's such a good word," you say, "and — just to sharpen it — that verse is actually about temptation. The Greek there is *peirasmos.* God absolutely gives us more than we can handle. That's the whole point. That's why we need each other." You say it warmly. You smile the whole time. The team lead takes it well. Some of them even nod, genuinely fed.

It works, barely. But three people privately decide never to volunteer a verse near you again, and you feel the exact half-inch the room pulled back. You won the point. You always win the point. You note the cost and pray about it later, longer than you meant to.

## D:lose

"Just to sharpen it," you say, "that verse is actually about temptation — the Greek is *peirasmos* —" and you watch the team lead's face fall in real time in front of all 47 of her peers. The room goes quiet in the bad way. You have corrected a volunteer's heartfelt verse during the pep talk, on a Sunday, in public, and everyone can feel that you were technically right and pastorally wrong.

Greg watches the whole thing. Greg has seen this exact move sink a culture before — it's part of why his last church imploded. "Can I give you one thing later?" he says quietly. You already know what it is. The team lead does not lead a huddle moment again for a year.

9:58 AM. First service. You're closing the worship set with a baby dedication — the Hendersons, their fourth, a child named, according to the card in your hand, Maverick Cannon Henderson. You make a private peace with the name. It is not your name to make peace with, but you make it anyway.

The family comes up. The lights are warm. The room is soft and ready, the way a room gets right before a baby. You take the card. You say the parents' names. You reach for the baby.

The baby has reviewed the situation and rendered a verdict. The baby begins to scream — not a fussy scream, a theological scream, the scream of a soul that did not consent to any of this. Four hundred people watch you, a microphone clipped to your collar, holding a furious infant named Maverick Cannon, with a full prayer still to deliver and the worship leader watching from the wings to see what you'll do.

## A

You take Maverick Cannon and tuck him against your chest in the football hold you perfected over four kids and nine hundred dedications. The screaming continues. You pray over it, calm and unbothered, raising your voice exactly enough. The room melts — there is nothing a congregation loves more than a pastor unbothered by a screaming baby. You hand him back at "amen." Mom mouths *thank you.* The screaming was the sermon.

## B

You leave Maverick Cannon right where he wants to be, in his mother's arms, and you lay one hand gently on the small furious head and pray over the whole family — the parents, the older three, the weight of a fourth. The baby keeps screaming. It doesn't matter. The prayer isn't for the baby. The baby will not remember this. The parents will remember every word. You aimed the moment at the people who would keep it.

## C

You speed up. You can feel yourself speeding up. The prayer that was going to be a benediction becomes a list of words said quickly near a child. You hand Maverick Cannon back at the first natural seam, three sentences early. The family doesn't notice. You notice. You wanted that baby off the stage more than you wanted to bless him, and the difference between those two things is the whole job, and you felt yourself land on the wrong side of it.

## D:win

You lean in. "I just — I sense something over this little one," you say, and the room leans with you. "I see a leader. I see a child who's going to walk into rooms and change the temperature." It is one hundred percent improvised and it is calibrated for any infant who has ever screamed in any sanctuary. The parents weep. The room weeps. Maverick Cannon, sensing he has won, stops crying at the exact right second, and four hundred people decide they just witnessed something. You hand him back a legend. You did, mostly, mean it.

## D:lose

You lean in for the prophetic word and reach, again, for the specific. "I see a worship leader," you say. "I see this child on a stage, leading thousands." The room loves it. The Hendersons love it less than you'd think, because — and you could not have known this — the Hendersons are a deeply tone-deaf family who have already privately decided this child will be an engineer, and you have just publicly assigned their newborn a career on a stage in front of four hundred witnesses. "We'll see," says Mr. Henderson into your hot mic, drily, and four hundred people hear him, and they laugh, and the laugh is at you.

Second service. The sermon landed. You move into the altar call — the part you'd never put in a brochure but is the actual point of all of it. The lights drop. Brody plays soft pads. People come forward. A young couple. A man you've prayed with before. A teenager, alone, which always undoes you a little.

Then a guest in row three rises. You've clocked him all morning — denim, a lot of rings, the posture of a man who arrived with a word. He moves to the front, but not to kneel. He turns, faces the congregation, raises a hand, and announces that the Lord has shown him "a spirit of heaviness over this house" and that he has "come to break it." He has a microphone presence he did not earn here. He is freelancing an exorcism at your altar call in front of your people, and forty heads have turned from the kneeling teenager to the man with the rings.

This is your house. This is your altar. This is happening live, on the livestream, which has, of course, chosen now to be working perfectly.

## A

You cross the stage unhurried, put a warm hand on his shoulder-blade, and lean into a shared mic. "Thank you, brother — we receive a heart for freedom here." And then, smoothly, to the room: "And right now I want us to keep our eyes on these few who came forward — let's surround them." You have not rebuked him. You have not embarrassed him. You have simply, gently, taken your house back, and the room exhales because the pastor has the wheel again. The teenager is still kneeling. You get to her. That was the whole point.

## B

You give him one sentence. "Amen — I believe God wants freedom for this house, always." You affirm the heart so he has somewhere to put the energy. Then: "And the way we break heaviness here is together, at this altar, with these folks who came forward. Come pray with us." You've handed him a role instead of a duel. He kneels. The moment reknits. Grace, it turns out, is also a de-escalation tactic, and you are fluent in both at once.

## C

You freeze. It's only two seconds but the room feels it. The man, sensing no resistance, downshifts into a longer gear — he begins naming "spirits," he begins pacing, he asks people to "stand if you feel it," and forty confused people half-stand. The altar call is now his. The teenager who came forward alone quietly returns to her seat, unprayed-for, because a stranger needed the room more than she did. You regain control eventually. "Eventually" is the saddest word in pastoring.

## D:win

Something competitive and ancient wakes up in you. You take the mic — *your* mic — and you go bigger. "I sense it too," you say, voice dropping into the register, "and I sense something even deeper — a Father in this room who is not heavy but *near,* who is calling some of you by name right now." You are, frankly, magnificent. You out-prophesy the prophet so warmly and so specifically-vaguely that the room swings entirely back to you and forgets the man with the rings ever stood up. People weep. The teenager comes back forward. It works completely, and on the drive home you'll examine the part of you that enjoyed winning a prophecy contest, and you won't love what you find.

## D:lose

You take the mic to out-prophesy him, full register, full warmth — "I sense something *deeper* —" and the man with the rings, who did not come here to be out-prophesied, turns and points at you and says, into the same sound system, "And the Lord shows me there is *pride* on this platform." The room goes electric-silent. He may be a freelancer. He is also, in this specific instant, not wrong, and everyone can feel that he's not wrong, and you are now in a public prophetic standoff that you started by trying to win one. Pastor Dave is in the back. Pastor Dave watched his campus pastor get into a spiritual duel at an altar call and lose the crowd's read on it. "Let's chat this week."

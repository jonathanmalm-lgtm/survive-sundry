1:15 PM. The lobby is thinning. And there, refilling the connection-card holder for the fourth time today, is Lyle.

Lyle is seventy-one. Lyle has greeted at this door longer than you've been ordained. Lyle is also, lovingly, one of your three. Lyle corners first-time guests for nine minutes about the parking situation. Lyle has made three families "feel weird" — that's the word that came in on the cards — and last week Lyle told a visiting young mom that her stroller was "a fire-code problem," which it was not, because Lyle is not the fire marshal, Lyle is a man who loves a rule.

The team has gently raised it. Twice. You've put it off twice, because Lyle's wife passed two years ago and this door is the reason Lyle gets out of bed on a Sunday, and you know that, and everybody knows you know that. This is the part of the job nobody dreams about when they feel the call. The list in the back of your head has had Lyle's name on it for a month, and you have never once been able to make the list mean it.

## A

You take Lyle to the quiet corner by the coat rack and you tell him the truth, the kind way, the slow way. That you love him. That the door has been his gift for a long time. That you want to move him somewhere his heart fits the room better. You don't dress it up and you don't dump it on him. Lyle's eyes go wet and so do yours, and he says, "I think I knew." It's the hardest four minutes of your day and the most pastoral thing you do all morning. You didn't outsource it to an email. You stayed in it. That's the whole job.

## B

"Lyle — not here, not squeezed between services. Can I take you to lunch Tuesday? I want to talk about your spot here, and I want to do it right." Lyle, who has braced for a brush-off, softens. Tuesday you'll thank him to his face, by name, with specifics — the families he's welcomed over fifteen years — before you ever get to the hard part. You've decided Lyle's exit will have at least as much dignity as Lyle's tenure. The decision waits. The respect doesn't.

## C

You make your rounds. You smile at Lyle. You let it ride. Future You can handle Lyle, and Future You, you've noticed, is a remarkably busy and conveniently unavailable man. Next Sunday Lyle tells a different young mom her diaper bag is "blocking an egress," and the card comes in Monday, and the team looks at you, and you look at the card, and you realize the only thing you accomplished by waiting was assigning the cost of your discomfort to a stranger with a diaper bag.

## D:win

You can't do it. You reach, instead, for invention. "Lyle," you say, "I've been praying about how to honor you, and I want to create a role: Greeter Emeritus. A statesman position. You'd mentor the new greeters." There is no such role. There is no such thing as a statesman position at a fourteen-hundred-person church. But Lyle's whole face lifts — *emeritus,* it sounds like something they put on a plaque — and he accepts with tears, and he steps back from the door he was making weird, and it works. It is a beautiful, cowardly, kind little lie, and it solves the actual problem, and you'll spend the drive home deciding whether "Greeter Emeritus" was grace or just conflict-avoidance with good production values. (It was both. It's always both.)

## D:lose

"I want to create a role for you," you say. "Greeter Emeritus." Lyle lights up — and immediately, because Lyle is a man who loves a structure, asks the questions a real role would have answers to. "Who do I report to? What's the mentoring schedule? Is there a budget for greeter training? Should I be at the staff meeting?" You do not have answers, because the role is a vapor you exhaled four seconds ago to avoid a hard conversation. Lyle, sensing the vapor, deflates, then hardens. "Are you trying to get me off the door?" he asks, loudly enough that two staff members turn. Now you're having the exact conversation you invented a fake job to avoid, except now it has an audience and a betrayal in it. Pastor Dave, by the auditorium doors, has heard the phrase "Greeter Emeritus" travel across the lobby and is walking over with a face that means he has questions about the org chart.

Third song. The tech leader's voice crackles in your earpiece: "Livestream's down. Hardware. I can't get it back before the message." Brody glances at you from the stage. The congregation has no idea — they're mid-chorus. But Greg, in the fourth row, just glanced at his phone, which means he watched the little stream icon go gray.

You have a joke you tell people: "I only really preach when the livestream goes down." It is no longer a joke. The sermon you've worked on for four days is in a folder on your phone and, more to the point, in your head. In thirty seconds you walk into a live room and preach to the people who are actually here, while the rest of the internet stares at a frozen frame of a worship pad.

There is also, you notice, a second sermon in your head — the one you've been carrying for three weeks. The one with teeth. The one you haven't preached because you weren't sure you wanted it on the internet forever, attached to your name, searchable by elders. And the internet, right now, is off.

## A

You walk out at the seam. Brody adds a verse to let you settle. You take the mic and preach the one you built — not your most polished of the year, but present in a way polished sometimes isn't. Greg finds you after. "You were ready." "I'm always supposed to be," you say. He nods, and writes nothing, which from Greg is the higher compliment.

## B

You give tech the two minutes. The feed doesn't come back. You walk out anyway, and the two minutes of pressure sharpened something — the sermon lands harder than the rehearsed version would have. The feed restores during the offering. Three people ask if the message will be online. It won't. You're surprisingly fine with that. Not everything has to be archived to have happened.

## C

Three minutes. Five. The tech leader texts "working on it." You stay in the lobby. Inside, the room can see the blank screen behind Brody, who is now on his fourth pass through the bridge and visibly running out of bridge. The congregation is kind but starting to wonder where their pastor is. You walk out at minute seven. Your entrance at minute seven carries less authority than it would have carried at minute zero. Everyone clocks the wait. Nobody says so.

## D:win

The internet is off, so you let yourself off the leash. You preach the three-week sermon — the honest one, the one with the hard turn you've been too cautious to put on tape — and without a camera in the room you find another gear. Two people stop you afterward to say it was the best thing they've heard you preach in a year. You don't tell them it was unplanned. You don't tell them you only dared because nobody was recording. You drive home and write it down properly, for the internet, where it will be twelve percent safer and eight percent worse.

## D:lose

You preach the riskier one, the one with teeth, and it has teeth, and one of the teeth bites a guy in row nine who came as a first-time guest with very strong opinions about that exact theological hill. He stands up. In the lobby afterward he wants to "dialogue," loudly, with the specific energy of a man who has been waiting his whole life for a pastor to say this out loud so he could object. You handle it. You handle it for nineteen minutes. The livestream comes back up just in time to not have recorded the good part and to fully record you cornered by row nine near the connection cards.

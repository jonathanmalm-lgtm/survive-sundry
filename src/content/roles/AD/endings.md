## Ending 1

Pastor Dave is at his desk. Not on his phone. Just looking at you. There's a banker's box on the corner. Your office keys and the key to the mechanical closet are on top of it. He lets the silence do the work, because he learned that from watching you do it. "We love you. We believe God has a plan for your life." A pause. "We've also brought someone in to take a fresh look at operations." The meeting takes nine minutes. On the way out, Brenda hands you a card from the dollar rack. Someone drew a little clipboard on it. Someone wrote "God has a plan!" You sit in your car. You look at the parking lot — the lot you opened, the cones, the gravel overflow. You know exactly how many spaces it holds. You pull out of one of them.

Real estate. It was always the fallback. Your first listing is Ron's rental property. Ron has a lot of feedback about the lockbox process. Ron keeps receipts.

## Ending 2

Pastor Dave slides a piece of paper across the desk. "Administrative Scheduling and Compliance Coordinator." Compliance. The word just sits there on the page, fluorescent. "Your attention to systems is honestly a gift," he says. "You're going to be great at identifying gaps." You drive home with the word *compliance* riding shotgun the whole way.

You will spend the next three years sending calendar reminders to people who do not read calendar reminders, and reconciling line items that other people created, including, in a turn you find genuinely funny on exactly one Tuesday a year, a legacy category called MISC — EVENT FLOW that nobody can explain and that you are now contractually required to investigate. Wendell cc's himself on several of your reminders. You never gave Wendell your work email. Wendell has it anyway.

## Ending 3

The meeting starts normally. Then the door opens. It's Tanner. He is 24. He has a podcast with 340 subscribers and a leather journal labeled "VISION DOWNLOAD" in large letters. He spent six months at Bethel and came back with a vocabulary that is mostly the word "activation." He sits down next to you. "I've had some initial thoughts about the budget," he says. "God's been speaking to me about *flow.*" You look at Pastor Dave. Pastor Dave is already looking at his phone.

Tanner's first directive is to "reallocate toward vision." His second is to ask whether there's any "discretionary margin" he could "steward for a content initiative." You feel, very specifically, the floor of EVENT FLOW shift beneath your feet. You say you'll "look into it." You will be looking into it for a long time.

## Ending 4

You're in the parking lot. Four minutes before the meeting. Your phone rings — unknown number, local area code. "This is Jason, I'm a pastor over at Redemption City. We've been looking for someone who actually understands operations — most of our growth right now is just chaos with good lighting." Of course you've heard of Redemption City. Endless budget. Real facilities team. A petty-cash process you've fantasized about. "We'd love to have a conversation. We can match what you're making and then some."

You walk into your meeting with Pastor Dave anyway. From your end the conversation is on autopilot; all you can hear is the words *real facilities team.* Wendell, in the lot, somehow already knows about the call. Wendell always knows. You take the meeting. You start at Redemption City in two months, where, you will discover, they have a category called "Contingency," and it is enormous, and it is fully approved, and you will weep a little the first time you see it.

## Ending 5

He's smiling when you walk in. The real one. "I've been doing this twenty-two years," he says. "Most people manage a Sunday. A few people hold one up so quietly you never see it happening. That was you today." He slides a notepad across — specific bullet points, in his handwriting: the cold lobby caught and explained, the overflow handled before it hit the road, the family that got seated who almost didn't, the volunteer you took care of. He noticed all of it. He noticed the things you spent the whole morning making sure nobody would notice.

"Well done. Good and faithful servant." He flips the notepad over. "Now — let's plan next Sunday." Wendell had filed a laminated report. It was positive. He brought a copy. Pastor Dave already had a copy.

## Ending 6

He walks you through a color-coded recap. The wins are green. The areas of growth are yellow. There is one red, and you know which one it is before his pen reaches it — the hallway, the bottleneck, the eleven minutes a first-time guest spent lost. He circles it once. "The team trusts you. The building runs because you run it. And you — you actually know where every gap is, which is rarer than you think." He shakes his head slowly, not in disappointment, in the way of a man recalibrating. "We'll do better next time."

The recap lives on your desk for three weeks. You look at the red circle every morning. Next to it, in your own hand, you've taped a connection card. It says "Google. Also the hallway was confusing." You keep them together. They keep you honest.

## Ending 7

The meeting goes well. Suspiciously well. He slides an envelope across the desk. The salary adjustment works out to approximately $83 a month. "It's not huge. But it's meaningful. It's a symbol." On the way home you stop at the grocery store. You stand in the pasta aisle for a long time. You pick up the store-brand ramen. You put it back. You pick up the Maruchan. You hold it for a moment, doing the math you always do, and you realize you can afford it now.

You frame the letter. It hangs a little crooked on the office wall. You notice it's crooked. You — the person who laminates, who levels, who measures twice — leave it crooked. It feels like the first thing you've let be imperfect in three years.

## Ending 8

He presents the new title. It is genuinely better — Director of Operations, all campuses. Then he presents the new responsibilities, and the list is long: "—facilities across both buildings, plus the third campus launching in the fall, plus vendor management, plus you'll own the full operating budget — and the budget itself stays flat for now, we'll revisit in—" he checks something, "—eighteen to twenty-four months." He closes the folder, beaming. "Congratulations. You earned this."

You shake his hand. You smile. You mean it. You get to your car. You open your calendar app. You look at it for a long time. You think about the third campus, and the second building, and the fact that there is still exactly one person who notices when the chair count is twelve short. You put the phone face-down on the seat. You sit there a minute. Then you drive home.

## Slush Fund Win

At one point in the meeting Pastor Dave pauses, taps a folder he doesn't open, and says, "Brenda mentioned the books closed clean this quarter. Cleaner than usual, actually." He studies you a beat longer than necessary. "Whatever you're doing back there — keep doing it." You hold his gaze and say, "Just good stewardship." He decides, visibly, not to pull that thread. The problem dissolved with no paper trail. The fund quietly regrew by Friday off a rounding you set in motion last month. On your way out, Wendell is in the lot. Wendell nods. You nod. Two men who know where everything in this building is buried, agreeing, without a word, never to dig. EVENT FLOW lives to smooth another Sunday. You are an unsung wizard. The church runs on a magic only you can see, and you intend to keep it that way.

## Slush Fund Lose

"One more thing," Pastor Dave says, and slides a printout across the desk. It's Brenda's reconciliation. There's a $4,200 discrepancy, and threaded all the way through it, like a signature, are your initials. "Help me understand," he says, very mildly, in the tone he saves for the things that are not mild at all, "what MISC — EVENT FLOW has been covering. For three years." You open your mouth. You have an explanation for everything. You have *always* had an explanation for everything — the cold lobby, the chairs, the vendor, the cash. You discover, sitting there, that you do not have a comfortable one for this. Brenda is not in the room. Brenda does not need to be. Brenda has the timestamps, the receipts, and a log she kept on the lid of a petty-cash box you never once thought of as something anyone else was watching. You will spend Tuesday writing a memo explaining a fund that only you understood. The church will get a real contingency line, fully approved, with two signatures required. Neither of them will be only yours ever again.

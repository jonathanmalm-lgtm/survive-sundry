Nine AM. The team huddle. You are here because you are always here, and you have been here since 7:15, and you will be here at the post-mortem too, bookending a day you are technically not responsible for.

The tech leader steps forward and "acknowledges some staffing gaps" with the haunted optimism of a man running on three hours of sleep. He quotes a verse: "God won't give you more than you can handle." That verse is about resisting temptation, not about being short two greeters. You have a note about this in a folder. This is not the moment for the note.

Pastor Dave produces the $5 Chick-fil-A card — staff consensus, "the volunteer closest to burnout." He calls Terry. Terry has a six-week-old at home and the thousand-yard stare of a man who has not slept since the baptism class. Terry came in anyway. Terry receives the card with the look of a person who did not know he was being watched and is undone, a little, that he was. The room claps. Terry blinks too much.

Then Greg is introduced. Former worship pastor at a megachurch that recently imploded over a "moral failure." Here to "observe and encourage." Excellent jacket. You like Greg fine. You also note, automatically, that Greg is in your building and Greg is not in your system, but that is a problem for a quieter minute.

And while everyone's heads are bowed for the donut prayer, you are the only one with your eyes open, and your open eyes are doing math, and the math says: Door 3 has nobody on it, doors open in eight minutes, and two hundred people are about to walk into an unmanned entrance and decide in four seconds whether this church is the kind of place that saw them coming.

## A

You wait for "amen" and then you say it, flat and fast: "Before everyone scatters — Door 3 has no coverage, doors are in seven, I need two hands." Two hands go up immediately because you asked plainly and people respond to plain. The door is covered before the prelude. Pastor Dave catches your eye on the way out and gives you a single nod, the cheap one, the one that costs him nothing and means everything, and you bank it, because banking those nods is most of your retirement plan.

## B

You don't interrupt the prayer. You are not going to be the administrator who steps on a tender moment for Terry to talk about a door. So you say nothing, and the prayer is lovely, and the huddle disperses, and Door 3 stands open and unmanned for the first nineteen minutes of service. Eleven people enter ungreeted. One is a first-time guest who later tells a friend the church felt "like a really nice building that wasn't expecting anyone." First Impressions reports "the door felt cold" at Hits & Misses. Nobody claims the door. You could claim the door. You don't.

## C

You let the prayer be the prayer. The second it ends, you intercept two reliable volunteers by the donut table — the ones who always say yes, which is its own quiet sin you'll repent of later — and you re-cover Door 3 yourself, walking the post for the first twelve minutes until relief arrives. You greet thirty people. You are good at it, surprisingly, warmly, for someone who'd describe himself as "back of house." Nobody in leadership ever knows the door was uncovered. The guests only know they were seen. That was you. It is always you.

## D:win

You text Pastor Dave under the table during the prayer: "Door 3 uncovered. Can you do a 30-sec 'who wants to serve today' from the stage before you preach?" Three dots. Then: "love it." At 9:52 he does it with the warm, gravitational authority only senior pastors have, and seven hands go up for a job that needs one. You now have a surplus of greeters and a line of people who feel chosen. Tom, already head usher, raises his hand too, because Tom cannot resist a moment. You do not acknowledge Tom's hand. Tom lowers it. Equilibrium is restored.

## D:lose

You text Pastor Dave under the table and Pastor Dave, reading on a stage between two thoughts, gets the gist exactly wrong. He announces from the platform that the church is "desperately short on volunteers across every ministry" and warmly directs anyone who feels called to "head to the connection card table after service to get plugged in." There is no one at the connection card table. There has never been anyone at the connection card table. Fourteen people migrate to it and form a loose, hopeful, leaderless cluster, waiting to be plugged in by nobody, until you sprint across the lobby to become, personally, the entire volunteer onboarding department. You are smiling the whole way. This is the job.

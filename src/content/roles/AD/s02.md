You are here at 7:30 for one reason, and the reason has a number, and the number is twelve. Sometime between last night's teardown and this morning, the Service 2 floor lost twelve folding chairs. You know this because you counted. You counted at 6:58, in the dark, alone, with a clicker, the way a man counts sheep he does not trust.

Twelve chairs is not a catastrophe. Twelve chairs is the difference between a full back row and a family of five standing along the wall pretending they prefer it. Twelve chairs is a connection card that says "couldn't find a seat." Twelve chairs is, more than anything, Tom.

Tom is the head usher. Tom owns the chair count the way other men own a boat. Tom's laminated floor plan says the room seats 214, and Tom has said the number 214 in three separate meetings, unprompted, with feeling. If the room comes up twelve short this morning, the question will not be *where did the chairs go.* The question will be *who can't count.* And the answer Tom has prepared, for three years, is not "Tom."

## A

You go to the fellowship-hall stack, pull twelve chairs, and walk them out two at a time because the cart squeaks and you do not have time to explain a squeak. You find the rhythm in ninety seconds — lift, step, set, step. It is meditative in the way all church labor is meditative: slightly undignified, completely necessary, witnessed by no one who will ever know it happened. The back row is whole by 8:05. The count is 214. Tom will arrive at 8:30 and find the room exactly as his floor plan promised, and he will feel, deep in his chest, briefly and wrongly, that *he* did that.

## B

You decide twelve chairs is a rounding error and proximity to God is not measured in folding chairs. This is theologically sound. It is operationally insane. Second service fills past the last full row, a family of five lines up against the east wall, a woman ends up sitting on the carpeted step, and Tom — Tom, who counts — does a slow visual sweep at 11:40 and *sees it.* Tom does not say anything to you. Tom says it to the room at Hits & Misses, where it is no longer twelve chairs. It is "an egress concern."

## C

You find Tom by the lobby doors and lay it out flat: "Service 2's twelve short. I think it walked off during teardown. You want to pull from fellowship or you want me to?" Tom straightens half an inch. This is the most anyone has acknowledged that the chair count is a real job with real stakes, and Tom has waited years for a colleague who understands the chair count is a real job with real stakes. "I'll handle it," Tom says, and he does, beautifully, and he tells you the corrected number at 8:40 unprompted, and for one Sunday you and Tom are not at war.

## D:win

You don't find twelve chairs. You make twelve chairs disappear *as a problem.* You quietly reset the four back rows from nine-wide to eight-wide, which opens the aisles, which Greg will later call "intentional," and which means the room now genuinely needs twelve fewer chairs to look full. The math closes. The room reads packed. Nobody counts a room that looks packed. The only casualty is that Tom's laminated 214 is now, technically, fiction — and when Tom mentions 214 in the meeting, you simply do not correct him, and only you know that the floor underneath his number quietly changed shape this morning while he was getting coffee.

## D:lose

You reset the back rows and the math closes and it would have worked, except Tom arrives early, walks his floor, and notices immediately that his rows are wrong, because Tom notices nothing in life except chairs and therefore notices *everything* about chairs. He re-counts. He gets a number that is not 214. He gets a number that is not *anyone's* number, because you changed the rows but he doesn't know the rows changed, so now there are two counts, both confident, both armed. Tom resets the rows back to nine-wide during the prelude. You are now twelve short again, on purpose, fifteen minutes before doors. Tom catches your eye across the room with the specific stare of a man who has found exactly one thing in this world he is good at and just watched someone move it.

Services are done. You're closing out the morning — work orders, deposit bags, the small administrative liturgy that ends every Sunday — and the building is finally going quiet. In the parking lot, Hector's white van eases out without a sound, the way it arrived, and you raise a hand, and he raises one back, and you think, again, that you genuinely do not know that man's last name and you have made peace with never knowing it.

Then Terry appears in your doorway. Terry of the $5 Chick-fil-A card, the six-week-old, the thousand-yard stare. Terry bought snacks for the volunteer table this morning — granola bars, a thing of clementines, the good coffee creamer instead of the powdered — because he's the kind of tired person who still thinks about other tired people. It came to about $80. He'd like it back.

He does not have the receipt. Of course he doesn't have the receipt. Terry has a newborn and a diaper bag and a brain currently operating on backup power; the receipt is in a car seat or a coat pocket or the void where new parents keep everything they will never see again. He's not asking for much. He's asking for the thing the policy exists to make complicated.

## A

You tell Terry, gently but clearly, that you can't reimburse without a receipt — that it's the policy, and that you wrote the policy, and that the policy exists precisely so that no volunteer ever has to wonder whether another volunteer is getting reimbursed for things that didn't happen. It is correct. It is defensible. It is also a tired man who bought clementines for strangers being told no, on his way home to a newborn, and the look on his face is going to live in your chest for a while. The books are clean. Something else isn't.

## B

You open the petty-cash box, count out $80, and hand it to Terry, and you do it before he's finished apologizing for the receipt, because the man bought clementines for the volunteer table on three hours of sleep and the policy can survive a single act of mercy. Then you do the actual administrator part: you walk him, kindly, through snapping a photo of the receipt next time, right there at the register, because protecting Terry includes protecting Terry from the version of this church that audits Terry. He nods. He'll remember. You log the $80 with a note. Clean and human, which is the rarest combination this job offers.

## C

You tell Terry to fill out the reimbursement form and submit it through the volunteer portal, and you watch something go out behind his eyes, because Terry knows what the portal is — the portal is where small kindnesses go to wait. The form needs the receipt he doesn't have. The portal needs your approval, which you will not get to for two weeks. Terry says "no worries" in the voice of a man who has many worries and is too polite to add yours to the pile. He may not buy the clementines next time. That's how this church slowly loses the Terrys: never with a no, always with a form.

## D:win

You pay Terry the $80 from petty cash on the spot — relief floods his exhausted face — and then you solve the receipt problem the way you solve everything, by relocating it: you file the $80 under "MISC — EVENT FLOW" as "guest hospitality," where it reconciles cleanly with no receipt required, because EVENT FLOW does not ask for receipts, EVENT FLOW asks no questions at all. Terry's taken care of. The books balance. The policy stays technically intact because the policy never has to know. It's the perfect small mercy: a kindness made invisible, paid for by a fund nobody audits. You are, once again, very good at this. You try not to notice how easy it's getting.

## D:lose

You pay Terry his $80 and file it under EVENT FLOW as "guest hospitality," which works beautifully and would have stayed buried forever, except that on your desk, in the stack of connection cards you collected from the lobby table, is one in a first-time guest's careful handwriting. Under "How did you hear about us?" she has written: "Google. Also the hallway was confusing." You stop. You read it twice. And it lands, all at once, that you have spent this entire morning becoming a virtuoso at hiding small problems inside a category nobody checks — the cold lobby, the bottleneck, the cash, now Terry's clementines — and that "the hallway was confusing" is the one problem you cannot reclassify, because she felt it, and she wrote it down, and there is no line item called EVENT FLOW for a guest who couldn't find the room. You keep the card. You keep all the cards. The cards are the one ledger you can't cook.

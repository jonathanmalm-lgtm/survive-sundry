Second service is running, which is the only reason you're free to be standing at the loading dock with Hector's cousin, who has just finished doing in forty minutes what the approved vendor wanted three days and $1,400 to do. The east-wing unit is purring. The lobby is a humane temperature. The morning is, against all odds, basically saved.

There is one catch, and the catch is standing in front of you wiping his hands on a rag. Hector's cousin — you got a first name, "Danny," you did not get a last name, you have learned not to want last names from anyone Hector sends — would like $600. In cash. Today. Now. "Hector said you're good for it," Danny says, which is both a compliment and a problem.

The check-writing process at this church takes a week, requires two signatures, and produces a paper trail with Danny's name on it, except Danny does not appear to have a name in the sense the paper trail means. The approved-vendor account is the *expensive* account and the slow one. And Danny is not going to come back next Sunday for a check. Danny fixed your church for a fraction of the real cost and Danny would like to be paid the way men like Danny are always paid, and the longer you stand here, the more this becomes a thing.

You happen to know there is exactly enough cash in the office safe to make Danny disappear in the most pleasant possible way.

## A

You tell Danny, kindly and immovably, that you can't do cash — that you need an invoice, a name, and a W-9, and you'll have a check cut by Friday. Danny does not love this. Danny calls Hector. Hector does not love this either; you can hear Hector not-loving-it through the phone. But you hold the line, because the line is the only thing standing between you and a category of decision you've watched end careers, and eventually Danny scrawls a last name you can't fully read and takes a photo of your W-9 with a deep sigh. It costs you the relationship a little. It keeps your initials off an envelope. Today, that's the trade.

## B

You tell Danny to have the office bill it Monday, and you turn to go back inside, and Danny just... stays. Danny is not a Monday-billing kind of operation. Danny leans on the dock and makes it clear, without raising his voice, that he is happy to wait right here, in the loading-dock doorway, in full view of the families now exiting second service, until somebody figures out how to pay him. You have created a standoff at the one door first-time guests use to leave with a good last impression. You will resolve this. You will resolve it worse, and later, and more expensively, than if you'd just decided something now.

## C

You take out your own wallet. You don't have $600 in your wallet — nobody has $600 in their wallet — but you have a banking app and Danny has a phone and a payment handle, and you send him $600 of your own money on the spot, and you tell yourself you'll file a reimbursement Monday. Danny is paid, Danny is gone, the dock is clear, the morning holds. The reimbursement is a real process you will navigate with real receipts, and it will mostly work, and the gap between "mostly" and "fully" is the quiet tax this church levies on the people who love it enough to cover for it with their own checking account.

## D:win

You walk to the office, open the safe, and there it is: the petty-cash box, fat and patient, fatter than petty cash has any right to be, because you have been feeding it for three years through roundings and reclassifications and one suspiciously generous category. You count out $600, slide it into an envelope, write "EVENT FLOW" on the front in your neat hand, and walk it back to the dock. Danny counts it, nods, vanishes. The dock is clear in ninety seconds. No invoice, no W-9, no week-long process, no paper with a half-legible last name. A problem that would have eaten your whole afternoon simply ceases to exist, dissolved in cash, off the books, the way the hardest problems always quietly dissolve. You feel like a wizard. Wizards do not get audited. Usually.

## D:lose

You count $600 out of the petty-cash box, write "EVENT FLOW" on the envelope, pay Danny, and Danny vanishes — flawless, clean, exactly the move. Except the petty-cash box has a log taped inside the lid, a log Brenda started keeping last month after the box "felt light," a log you forgot about because you have never once thought of that box as something anyone else watches. The box is now $600 lighter with no countersigned slip. Brenda reconciles petty cash on Tuesdays. Brenda is going to find an envelope's worth of nothing where $600 used to be, and the only note in the entire system is the word "EVENT FLOW," written, unmistakably, in your handwriting.

First service is preaching. You are in the lobby doing lobby things — watching the doors, being available for the problems that didn't fit anywhere else — when Ron finds you, and Ron has the specific patient stillness of a man who has been waiting for service to start so the lobby would be quiet enough for this exact conversation.

Ron has been here since the church met in a school cafeteria. Ron tithes, monthly, an amount that is — and Ron is aware you are aware of this — almost exactly your salary. Ron has never once made it weird. Ron is about to make it a little weird.

"The building fund," Ron says, warmly. "We raised it for the east-wing expansion. That was three years ago. I've been watching the parking lot. There's no expansion." He's not angry. Anger you could deflect. Ron is *curious,* and Ron is *organized,* and Ron mentions, gently, that he keeps his own records, and that the quarterly statements the church publishes have gotten "a little less detailed lately." He'd just like to understand where the money went. He thinks, as the person who runs the operation, you'd want to know he's asking.

You do want to know. You also know exactly where some of that money went, and a small, specific portion of it currently lives in a line item Ron would find very interesting.

## A

You pull up the building-fund ledger on your phone — the real one, the clean one, the one you can defend — and you walk Ron through it line by line, standing right there in the lobby, because the fastest way to end a transparency question is total transparency. The expansion got delayed by a permit fight and a contractor who walked; the money's sitting in a restricted account, untouched, earning a little interest, exactly where it should be. Ron reads every line. Ron nods. "Thank you," he says, and he means it, and he tells four people over the next month that the church's books are clean, and Ron's word at this church is worth more than any audit.

## B

You tell Ron, honestly, that he's asking a fair question and it deserves more than a lobby and a sermon playing in the background. You take his questions in writing — he has them written down already, of course he does — and you put a real meeting on the calendar for Tuesday, ledger open, nothing hidden. Ron is disarmed by being taken seriously, which is, you've noticed, all most people at this church actually want. You'll keep the Tuesday meeting. You'll show him the real numbers. Ron will leave that meeting an ally for life, which is the most valuable thing a founding tither can become.

## C

You tell Ron, politely, that budget questions are really an elder-board matter and you'd hate to misspeak, and you steer the conversation toward the weather and the donuts. It works, in that Ron stops asking. It fails, in every other way. Ron came to *you* because Ron trusts you more than he trusts the elder board, and you have just quietly told the church's biggest giver that the person who runs the money would rather not discuss the money. Ron files that. Ron files everything. The next quarterly statement, Ron reads with a pen in his hand.

## D:win

You put a warm hand on the situation and tell Ron that every dollar is accounted for, the expansion's just tied up in permitting, and the books are in good shape — all of which is *true,* technically, comprehensively, as long as nobody opens the line called "MISC — EVENT FLOW" and asks why a building-fund operation has a discretionary category that breathes. Ron believes you, because you are believable, because you have spent three years being the most reliable man in the building. He pats your shoulder and walks back into service reassured. You have bought peace with your own credibility. It's a good trade today. It is a loan, though, and you know it, and somewhere a balance is quietly accruing interest you did not budget for.

## D:lose

You reassure Ron that everything's accounted for, and Ron, reassured, says one more friendly thing on his way out: "Great — then you won't mind if I request the detailed line items? I'll just email Brenda. She's so organized." And you feel it happen in real time, the floor going soft, because Brenda *is* so organized, and Brenda will pull the detailed line items, and the detailed line items include EVENT FLOW, and Brenda has been wanting an excuse to ask about EVENT FLOW for a while now. You smile and say "of course." It is the least sincere "of course" of your entire career, and Ron, who reads people for a living somehow, notices that it is.

First service is in full swing, which means you finally have a window to breathe, which means the radio crackles. It's Wendell, and Wendell never crackles for nothing. "Lot's full. Still coming." Four words. From Wendell, four words is a siren.

You step outside and confirm it instantly: every striped space is taken, a line of cars is idling at the entrance like a held breath, and three of them have their blinkers on in the universal language of *I am about to do something to your grass.* It's a bigger crowd than the count predicted. Bigger crowds are good. Bigger crowds are also forty cars with nowhere to go and a first-time family in a minivan deciding, right now, whether "we couldn't even park" is the story they tell about today.

This is, technically, Wendell's domain. Wendell owns the cones the way Tom owns the chairs. But the cones can only point at spaces that exist, and right now you are forty spaces short of grace.

## A

You jog to the back lot — the unstriped gravel one nobody uses because it floods and the line of sight to the door is bad — and you open it, and you grab the hand-painted OVERFLOW → sign out of the storage closet where you have kept it, ready, for exactly this, since 2011. You plant it at the entrance and wave the idling line back. Forty cars find gravel. The minivan family parks, walks the slightly-too-long walk, and gets greeted warmly at a door you made sure was covered. Nobody in that building will ever know the lot overflowed. You will know. You'll be wiping gravel-mud off the sign Monday, alone, satisfied.

## B

You decide the lot is Wendell's and the cones are Wendell's and you are not going to insert yourself into a domain that already has an oracle. You go back inside. This is defensible. It is also the one Sunday Wendell could've used a second body, and he doesn't get one, and the line at the entrance backs into the actual road, and someone honks during the offering — you can hear it from the lobby, a car horn, under a prayer about generosity. First Impressions reports "parking felt chaotic." Wendell says nothing at the meeting. Wendell's silence is its own laminated report.

## C

You go out and you back Wendell up, and Wendell gives you the look — the rare one, the one that says he sees you saw — and the two of you run the overflow as a unit, you on the gravel lot and the sign, Wendell threading the entrance line with the cones, wordless, synchronized, two men who run the back of the same house. Forty cars land. The road clears. Nobody honks during the offering. Afterward Wendell finds you, and he doesn't thank you, because Wendell doesn't thank, but he gives you one slow nod, and you understand you have just been promoted in the only org chart that has ever actually mattered at this church.

## D:win

You don't make new spaces. You *borrow* them. The insurance office next door shares a fence and a gate, and it's Sunday, and they're closed, and the gate has a chain but the chain has a combination and the combination is, hilariously, the same one it's been since the fence went up. You open it. You walk the OVERFLOW sign over and point forty cars into a beautiful, empty, freshly-sealed lot that belongs to people who will never, ever know. The crowd parks. The road clears. The minivan family is seated before the second song. You close the gate after last service, re-chain it exactly as it was, and the only evidence is forty grateful families who think this church has a lot twice the size it has.

## D:lose

You open the neighbor's gate and point forty cars into the insurance office's pristine lot, and it works perfectly, right up until the insurance office is, it turns out, *not* closed — there's a Sunday claims adjuster doing month-end, and he comes out at 11:15 to find his lot full of Execution Church and a hand-painted sign with your church's name pointing directly at it. He photographs the sign. He photographs the cars. He emails the church office a very calm note about "unauthorized use" and "liability," CC'ing, for reasons you'll never fully understand, the city. Pastor Dave forwards you the email Monday with no words, just the email, which from Pastor Dave is the longest sentence there is.

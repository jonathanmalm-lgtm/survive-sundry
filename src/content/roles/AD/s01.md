You arrive at 6:58 because 7:00 is late and you've known this since 2011. The lobby smells like carpet cleaner and something underneath the carpet cleaner — warm, mechanical, wrong. You find the source in nine seconds, because finding the source is what you do. The HVAC unit above the east wing is making a noise that HVAC units are not built to make. Wet. Rhythmic. Clicking. You have heard this exact noise before. The last time you heard it, it cost $4,200, and the invoice is still laminated in a binder, because you laminate the things that hurt.

The thermostat reads 62°F. It is March. The unit is running and producing nothing, which is the most expensive thing a unit can do. You check the facilities log on your clipboard. The last note reads "looked fine" and is dated fourteen months ago. The person who wrote that note no longer works here. You are the person who works here.

First service is in three hours. Two hundred people will sit in this room. Pastor Dave will not learn this happened until it is already fixed or already a disaster. There is no third option. That is the job.

## A

You call the vendor on the approved list. They can have someone out by 10:30, which is the middle of first service, which is useless, but it's documented and it's correct and the work order has a number. You also crack the rooftop access and reset the unit yourself, which you are absolutely not certified to do, which is why you do it quietly. It catches. The lobby climbs to 68°F by 9:40. The vendor invoices you anyway, for "diagnostic." You pay it. Nobody knows the room was ever cold. That's the job.

## B

You write "HVAC — EAST UNIT — CLICKING — ASAP" on a sticky note in your good handwriting and press it to the thermostat. You return to your clipboard. By 9:20 a volunteer named Brenda has removed your sticky note because it "looked messy" and "we have guests." The sanctuary hits 59°F during worship. Three people leave. One of them tweets "ice church 🥶." The tweet gets fourteen likes. You will be handed all fourteen of those likes at the Hits & Misses meeting, by name, with a printout.

## C

Wendell sees you crossing the lot at 7:01 and gives you a slow, weighted thumbs-up before you've made a sound, because Wendell is always already there. You explain the clicking. He tilts his head a quarter inch. "East unit?" "East unit." "That's a capacitor." He produces a phone older than the building and calls a man named Hector. Hector arrives at 8:10 in a white van with no logo and no plate you'd want to memorize. The unit purrs by 8:35. You pay Hector's cousin in cash because Hector "doesn't do invoices on Sundays." You never learn Hector's last name. You will not ask.

## D:win

You spend forty-five minutes relocating signage, dragging check-in tables, and informing every single volunteer, with the flat authority of a person reading a verdict, that the fellowship hall "has always had better acoustics anyway." This is not true. There are no acoustics in the fellowship hall. But you say it the way you say everything, and within twenty minutes three volunteers are repeating it to first-time guests as a *feature* — "yeah, we love this room, the acoustics" — and the lie becomes a selling point you no longer have to maintain. The east wing stays sealed. Everyone assumes it's intentional. In the H&M meeting Pastor Dave compliments "the flow." You accept the compliment on behalf of a broken capacitor.

## D:lose

On paper the pivot is elegant. In execution, the fellowship-hall check-in dumps everyone into a single doorway, the coffee line and the lanyard line cross like two rivers that hate each other, two families end up in the wrong service entirely, and a first-time guest spends eleven minutes alone in a hallway before a single human acknowledges her existence. She fills out a connection card anyway, because she is a better person than this church deserves today. Under "How did you hear about us?" she writes: "Google. Also the hallway was confusing." You find the card Monday morning. You do not throw it away. You keep it in the binder, next to the $4,200 invoice, in the place where you keep the things that hurt.
